# Congestion and potential games

<p class="lead">In a congestion game, each player picks resources and pays a cost that rises with how many others share them. These games always have a pure Nash equilibrium, and simple best-response dynamics reach it, because every improving move decreases a single potential function that cannot fall forever.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each time a driver switches to a cheaper road, they lower their own cost, and it turns out that this also lowers a global quantity, the potential, by exactly the same amount. Since the potential is bounded below and drops with every improving move, the process cannot go on forever, and where it stops, no one can improve, which is a Nash equilibrium. The potential is a ledger that turns many selfish moves into a single decreasing number.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="congestion and potential games">
In a congestion game, each resource has a cost that depends only on how many players use it, and a player's cost is the sum over the resources it picks. Rosenthal showed every such game admits an exact potential $\Phi$: whenever one player switches, the change in $\Phi$ equals the change in that player's cost. Because $\Phi$ strictly decreases at every improving move and takes finitely many values, best-response dynamics converge to a pure Nash equilibrium. Monderer and Shapley showed congestion games and finite potential games are essentially the same class.
</DefinitionAside>

Here $\Phi(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{x} k + 3(4 - x) = \tfrac{x(x+1)}{2} + 3(4-x)$ when $x$ drivers use road A. Evaluating: $\Phi(0) = 12$, $\Phi(1) = 10$, $\Phi(2) = 9$, $\Phi(3) = 9$, $\Phi(4) = 10$. The minimum is at $x = 3$ (and $x = 2$), and $x = 3$ is a Nash equilibrium: each road costs $3$, so no driver gains by switching. Best-response dynamics walk $\Phi$ downhill to that rest point.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Follow the potential downhill">
Start with everyone on road B: $x = 0$, and $\Phi(0) = 0 + 3\cdot 4 = 12$. Road A costs $1$ to the first mover versus $3$ on B, so a driver switches; now $x = 1$, $\Phi(1) = 1 + 9 = 10$, down by $2$, exactly that driver's saving ($3 \to 1$).

Another driver finds A (now costing $2$ if they join, making it $3$) still no worse than B's $3$, and switches; $x = 2$, $\Phi = 3 + 6 = 9$. A third does the same to reach $x = 3$, where A costs $3$, equal to B: no one gains by moving. $\Phi(3) = 6 + 3 = 9$, the minimum. The dynamics halt at the pure Nash equilibrium, guaranteed by the ever-decreasing potential.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Congestion games always have a pure Nash equilibrium and always reach it by best response, because an exact potential function decreases at every improving move and cannot fall forever.</Callout>
</Section>
