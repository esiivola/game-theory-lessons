# Mixed strategies in sport

<p class="lead">This is the mixed-strategy lesson applied where the stakes are real and the data exist. Penalty kicks, tennis serves, pitch selection: in each, one side wants to be unpredictable at the frequencies that keep the other side guessing. The right mix is usually not 50/50, and a keeper who reads you turns any pattern into a save.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Your natural side scores more, so favoring it looks smart, until the keeper adjusts and covers it. Then the whole point of the mix is defeated. The unexploitable mix is the one where the keeper cannot do better by favoring either side, and because the sides are not equally dangerous, that mix is tilted rather than even. Any predictable habit, always going natural, alternating, following a streak, hands the keeper a read.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Take the scoring rates estimated from professional kicks (Palacios-Huerta). Each entry is the kicker's chance of scoring, given the side kicked and the side the keeper dives.

<ExhibitCaption n={2} text="Scoring probability (kicker's chance)" />

| Kick \ Keeper dives | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| **Left** | 0.60 | 0.95 |
| **Right** | 0.90 | 0.70 |

This is (almost) zero-sum: what the kicker gains in scoring, the keeper loses in save rate. Let the kicker shoot Left with probability $p$. The keeper compares diving Left against diving Right, and the kicker picks $p$ to make the keeper indifferent:

$$
\underbrace{0.60\,p + 0.90(1-p)}_{\text{keeper dives Left}} = \underbrace{0.95\,p + 0.70(1-p)}_{\text{keeper dives Right}}.
$$

Solving, $0.90 - 0.30p = 0.70 + 0.25p$, so $p = \tfrac{0.20}{0.55} = \tfrac{4}{11} \approx 0.36$. At that mix the scoring rate is about $0.79$, and the keeper cannot raise its save rate by leaning either way. Shoot Left far more or far less than $36\%$ of the time and a sharp keeper cuts you below $0.79$.

<DefinitionAside label="the exploitable pattern">
Unpredictable does not mean even. It means no strategy the opponent can adopt beats their equilibrium response. A fixed bias is exploitable, and so is any deterministic rule (alternating, copying your last make), because a rule can be learned. Only a mix at the right frequencies, drawn fresh each time, is safe.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Find the mix, then see the cost of a habit">
Set the keeper's two options equal: $0.60p + 0.90(1-p) = 0.95p + 0.70(1-p)$. The left side falls in $p$ (favoring Left helps the Left-diving keeper), the right side rises. They meet at $p = 4/11 \approx 0.36$, where scoring is $0.60(0.36) + 0.90(0.64) \approx 0.79$.

Now suppose you always take your natural side (say Right). The keeper learns this and always dives Right, holding you to $0.70$ instead of $0.79$. The predictable habit costs you nine goals in a hundred. Randomizing at the right rate is worth more than picking your best-looking shot every time.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="reality">
## A note from the real world

<Callout kind="reality-check">
The theory holds up outside the classroom. Studying professional penalties, Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002) and Palacios-Huerta (2003) found that top players mix at close to the equilibrium frequencies and show no serial pattern a keeper could exploit. The best in the world really do play minimax. The Behavioral unit later asks where this discipline holds and where it breaks down.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">The winning mix equalizes the opponent's options, which is usually not 50/50, and it has to be genuinely random. Any pattern, however clever, is a pattern the other side can learn.</Callout>
</Section>
