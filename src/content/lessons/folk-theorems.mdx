# Folk theorems

<p class="lead">The last lesson showed cooperation can survive if players are patient. The folk theorem says far more, and it is a warning as much as a result: when the future matters enough, nearly every outcome that clears a low bar can be held up as an equilibrium. Repetition explains too much.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The engine is the threat. Any deviation can be met with punishment that drives the deviator down to their minmax, the most they can guarantee themselves while everyone else pushes them as low as they can. As long as the target leaves both players above that floor, the loss from being punished outweighs the one-time gain from cheating, so nobody deviates. Patience makes the future punishment loom large enough to hold almost any arrangement in place.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

The feasible payoffs are the convex hull of the stage-game outcomes: for the PD with $T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0$, the hull of $(3,3), (5,0), (0,5), (1,1)$. A player's **minmax** is the lowest payoff the others can force on them while the player best-responds; in the PD that is $P = 1$ (defect against a defector).

<DefinitionAside label="folk theorem">
For an infinitely repeated game, take any feasible payoff profile in which every player earns strictly more than their minmax value. Then for discount factors close enough to 1, there is a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the repeated game whose average payoff is exactly that profile. This guarantees the strict interior of the feasible, individually rational region. Boundary points need separate analysis: some are equilibria too, but the strict theorem does not settle them. (With two players that is the whole statement; with three or more, Fudenberg and Maskin also need the payoff set to be full-dimensional, so that a deviator can be punished without punishing the punishers.)
</DefinitionAside>

So $(3,3)$ is an equilibrium, but so is $(3.5,2)$, and so are countless asymmetric splits. Two flavors: the Nash-threat version (Friedman) punishes with a stage-game Nash equilibrium; the stronger minmax version (Fudenberg-Maskin) punishes down to the minmax, which enlarges the supportable set.

The lesson is sobering. Repetition does not pick out cooperation; it makes prediction hard, because so many outcomes are consistent with equilibrium.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Support a point, and check the floor">
**Symmetric.** To hold $(3,3)$, both play grim trigger: cooperate, and defect forever if the other ever does. A one-time defection gains $5 - 3 = 2$ now but forfeits the cooperative stream, so for $\delta$ high enough nobody deviates. Standard cooperation.

**Asymmetric.** To hold $(3.5, 2)$, alternate outcomes over time (say, favouring player 1 in some rounds) so the long-run averages land there, and back it with the threat of minmax punishment. Both averages exceed the minmax of 1, so each player prefers the arrangement to being punished, and the profile is supportable.

**The floor.** Try $(0.5, 0.5)$: both are below the minmax of 1. A player can guarantee 1 by simply defecting forever, so no equilibrium can pin them at 0.5. The individually rational bound is what rules a target out.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">With patient players, every feasible payoff above the minmax is an equilibrium of the repeated game. The folk theorem does not predict cooperation; it shows repetition can rationalize almost anything.</Callout>
</Section>
