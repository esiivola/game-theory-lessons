# Rationality and common knowledge

<p class="lead">Being rational is not enough to know what to do. You also need a view about how the other players think, and about how they think you think. This game makes that chain visible.</p>

<Section id="ladder">
## The reasoning ladder

If everyone chose at random, the average would be about 50, so two-thirds of it is about 33. But if everyone is smart enough to see that, they all aim for 33, and now two-thirds of the average is 22. Keep going and the chain marches down toward 0.

<Callout kind="ask">
So should you guess 0? Only if you believe everyone else will reason all the way down too, and believe that they believe it, and so on. That endless "everyone knows that everyone knows" is called common knowledge of rationality. Real people stop after a step or two, so the winning number is usually around 20 to 33, not 0.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Walk the ladder">
Level 0 plays randomly, average $50$. Level 1 best-responds: $\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 50 = 33$. Level 2 best-responds to that: $\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 33 = 22$. The unique Nash equilibrium is $0$, reached only in the limit. Nagel (1995) found real players cluster at $33$ and $22$, that is, one and two steps of reasoning.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Rationality alone does not pin down a choice. What pins it down is a shared, and often wrong, assumption about how deeply everyone else reasons.</Callout>
</Section>
