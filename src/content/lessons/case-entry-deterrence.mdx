# Entry deterrence in business

<p class="lead">This is subgame perfection and commitment applied to industrial strategy. A bare threat to fight entry is not credible, so it does not deter. But a sunk investment, like building excess capacity, can change the incumbent's own payoffs so that fighting becomes profitable, and the threat becomes real.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Once the entrant is in, the incumbent compares two futures: fight and earn 20, or accommodate and earn 50. It accommodates, so the entrant safely enters. Announcing "I will fight" changes nothing, because the entrant looks ahead and sees the incumbent would not. Deterrence requires changing the post-entry incentives before the fact. Extra capacity can lower the marginal cost of producing aggressively, making fighting more attractive than accommodating. Its strategic effect comes from that changed marginal incentive, not from the investment merely being sunk.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real strategy

<DefinitionAside label="commitment through sunk investment (Dixit)">
Deterrence requires a credible threat, and subgame perfection says a threat is credible only if the incumbent would prefer to carry it out after entry. Dixit (1980) showed how pre-entry capacity can change this: extra capacity may lower the marginal cost of producing a lot, which can make aggressive post-entry output more attractive. Calling the expenditure sunk is not enough. The investment deters only if it changes the incumbent's later best response.
</DefinitionAside>

Without commitment, post-entry payoffs are Accommodate $50 >$ Fight $20$, so the incumbent accommodates and entry occurs; the incumbent earns $50$ instead of its monopoly $100$. Now suppose capacity changes production costs enough to raise the fight payoff above the accommodate payoff. Then fighting is the credible best response, the entrant foresees a loss from entering, and stays out. The capacity expenditure itself must still be subtracted when deciding whether deterrence is profitable overall.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="From empty threat to credible deterrence">
Solve the tree without commitment. At the incumbent's node, Accommodate pays $50$ and Fight pays $20$, so it accommodates. The entrant, foreseeing $40$ from entering versus $0$ from staying out, enters. The incumbent is stuck at $50$, and its threat to fight was never credible.

Now sink an investment in capacity that makes producing aggressively cheap, so that after entry, fighting yields, say, $60$ while accommodating still yields $50$. Now the incumbent's best response to entry is to fight, and the entrant, foreseeing a loss of $-10$, stays out. The incumbent keeps its monopoly. The investment worked not by being used (fighting never happens on the equilibrium path) but by making the threat credible, exactly the value of commitment.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">A bare threat to fight entry is not credible and does not deter. Pre-entry capacity can deter only when it changes later production incentives enough to make fighting the incumbent's best response. Sunkness alone does not create commitment.</Callout>
</Section>
