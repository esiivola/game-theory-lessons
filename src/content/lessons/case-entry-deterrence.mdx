# Entry deterrence in business

<p class="lead">This is subgame perfection and commitment applied to industrial strategy. A bare threat to fight entry is not credible, so it does not deter. But a sunk investment, like building excess capacity, can change the incumbent's own payoffs so that fighting becomes profitable, and the threat becomes real.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Once the entrant is in, the incumbent compares two futures: fight and earn 20, or accommodate and earn 50. It accommodates, so the entrant safely enters. Announcing "I will fight" changes nothing, because the entrant looks ahead and sees the incumbent would not. The way to deter is to change your own payoffs before the fact: sink money into capacity that only pays off if you produce aggressively, so that after entry, fighting actually earns more than accommodating. Now the threat is credible, and the entrant stays out.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real strategy

<DefinitionAside label="commitment through sunk investment (Dixit)">
Deterrence requires a credible threat, and subgame perfection says a threat is credible only if the incumbent would prefer to carry it out after entry. Dixit (1980) showed how a sunk investment in capacity changes this: by pre-building capacity, the incumbent lowers its marginal cost of producing a lot, so a post-entry price war becomes profitable rather than ruinous. The investment is valuable precisely because it removes the incumbent's temptation to accommodate.
</DefinitionAside>

Without commitment, post-entry payoffs are Accommodate $50 >$ Fight $20$, so the incumbent accommodates and entry occurs; the incumbent earns $50$ instead of its monopoly $100$. Now suppose a sunk capacity investment raises the fight payoff above the accommodate payoff (say fighting now yields more than accommodating because the capacity is already paid for). Then fighting is the credible best response, the entrant foresees a loss from entering, and stays out, letting the incumbent keep its monopoly. Paradoxically, spending money to make one option better can be worth more than the flexibility it destroys.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="From empty threat to credible deterrence">
Solve the tree without commitment. At the incumbent's node, Accommodate pays $50$ and Fight pays $20$, so it accommodates. The entrant, foreseeing $40$ from entering versus $0$ from staying out, enters. The incumbent is stuck at $50$, and its threat to fight was never credible.

Now sink an investment in capacity that makes producing aggressively cheap, so that after entry, fighting yields, say, $60$ while accommodating still yields $50$. Now the incumbent's best response to entry is to fight, and the entrant, foreseeing a loss of $-10$, stays out. The incumbent keeps its monopoly. The investment worked not by being used (fighting never happens on the equilibrium path) but by making the threat credible, exactly the value of commitment.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">A bare threat to fight entry is not credible and does not deter. A sunk investment that makes fighting the incumbent's profitable response turns the threat real, so the entrant stays out, and commitment beats flexibility.</Callout>
</Section>
