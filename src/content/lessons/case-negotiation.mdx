# Bargaining and negotiation

<p class="lead">Real negotiation, a labour contract, a house sale, a merger, is alternating offers over a pie that decays with delay. Rubinstein's model turns that into a sharp prediction: the split depends on patience and on each side's outside option (BATNA), not on who talks loudest.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each side can reject and counter, but rejecting costs a round of shrinkage. A patient player (delta near 1) barely minds waiting, so they can hold out for more; an impatient player (low delta) settles quickly and cheaply. The subgame-perfect split balances these exactly. Outside options set the floor: no one accepts less than their BATNA, so a better alternative pulls the split your way, and a worse one pushes it away. Strikes and lockouts are the BATNA made visible, the costly disagreement point that disciplines both sides. When information is symmetric, rational players settle immediately; strikes happen mostly because of asymmetric information about what the other side can bear.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real strategy

<DefinitionAside label="Rubinstein alternating offers, and Nash bargaining">
In Rubinstein's model two players alternate offers over a pie of size 1, discounting the future by delta each round. The unique subgame-perfect equilibrium gives the proposer $1/(1+\delta)$ and the responder $\delta/(1+\delta)$, agreed in the first round. As $\delta \to 1$ this approaches the 50/50 Nash bargaining solution. Outside options (BATNAs) truncate the range: if a side can walk away for a known value, no deal below it is accepted, which shifts the equilibrium split toward whoever has the better alternative.
</DefinitionAside>

At $\delta = 0.9$ the proposer gets $1/1.9 \approx 0.526$ and the responder $0.9/1.9 \approx 0.474$. The first-mover advantage is real but small, and it shrinks to nothing as both sides grow patient. Change a BATNA and the whole split moves: improve your outside option and you capture more; worsen it and you concede. That is why negotiators spend more effort improving their alternatives than polishing their arguments. Strikes are not proof that bargaining is irrational; with full information the parties would settle at once. They arise when each side is unsure of the other's true reservation value and testing that costs a round of the shrinking pie.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Patience, BATNA, and the split">
Start with no outside options and $\delta = 0.9$. The proposer offers themselves $1/(1+\delta) = 0.526$ and the responder $0.474$, and the responder accepts, because rejecting and countering next round would give them only $0.526$ of a pie already shrunk by $0.9$, which is worse. As $\delta \to 1$, both approach $0.5$: patience erases the first-mover edge.

Now give the responder a BATNA of $0.4$: an outside deal worth $0.4$ if talks collapse. Any split offering them less than $0.4$ is rejected, so the equilibrium share cannot fall below $0.4$; the responder's slice rises toward their alternative and the proposer concedes. Improve the responder's BATNA further and they capture still more. A strike is this outside option in action: by absorbing the cost of no agreement, a side signals what it can endure, and the settlement moves accordingly, most sharply when neither is sure of the other's limit.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">In alternating-offers bargaining the split is set by patience and by each side's BATNA, not by who moves first or argues hardest. The first-mover edge fades as both grow patient, a better outside option wins a better deal, and strikes come from asymmetric information, not irrationality.</Callout>
</Section>
