# Replicator dynamics

<p class="lead">The ESS asks where a population rests. Replicator dynamics asks how it moves: the share of a strategy grows in proportion to how much its fitness beats the average. Sometimes it converges, and sometimes, as in Rock-Paper-Scissors, it circles forever without settling.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Whatever is common gets beaten: lots of Rock invites Paper, which invites Scissors, which invites Rock again. So no strategy can hold a lead, and the population chases its own tail around the even mix. With fair ties the chase is a perfect closed loop. Change the tie payoff and you tilt the balance: penalizing ties makes matching the crowd costly, nudging the population inward to the stable center; rewarding ties does the reverse and flings it toward the boundary.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="replicator dynamics">
Let $x_i$ be the share of strategy $i$ and $f_i$ its expected fitness against the current population. The replicator equation is $\dot{x}_i = x_i\,(f_i - \bar{f})$, where $\bar{f} = \sum_j x_j f_j$ is average fitness: strategies above average grow, those below shrink. Its rest points include every Nash equilibrium, and stable rest points correspond to evolutionarily stable strategies.
</DefinitionAside>

For fair Rock-Paper-Scissors (win $+1$, lose $-1$, tie $0$) the game is zero-sum, so average fitness is zero and the dynamics conserve a quantity: the trajectories are closed orbits circling the interior equilibrium $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$, which is neutrally stable, neither attracting nor repelling.

Give ties a payoff $e$. A negative $e$ penalizes playing like the crowd, favours diversity, and turns the orbits into an inward spiral toward the center, now asymptotically stable. A positive $e$ rewards conformity and spirals the population outward toward the edges, cycling between near-pure phases. Adding the Hawk-Dove game instead, the replicator converges to the ESS Hawk fraction $V/C$, linking this lesson to the last.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Read off the direction of motion">
Take the population $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ for (Rock, Paper, Scissors) with fair ties. A strategy's fitness is its expected score against this mix: Rock scores $x_S - x_P = 0.2 - 0.3 = -0.1$, Paper scores $x_R - x_S = 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3$, Scissors scores $x_P - x_R = 0.3 - 0.5 = -0.2$.

Paper is above the average, so its share grows; Rock and Scissors shrink. But as Paper rises, Scissors (which beats Paper) starts to pay, and the lead rotates. That rotation, with no strategy able to keep its advantage, is the cycle. With fair ties the loop is closed; a small tie penalty would instead pull each loop a little tighter until the population spirals into the even mix.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Replicator dynamics grow strategies that beat the average. In fair Rock-Paper-Scissors the population cycles forever on closed orbits; a small tie penalty stabilizes the even mix, a reward destabilizes it.</Callout>
</Section>
