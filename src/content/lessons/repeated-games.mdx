# Repeated games and discounting

<p class="lead">Played once, the Prisoner's Dilemma ends in mutual defection: betrayal is dominant. Played over and over, the calculation changes. A defection can be punished tomorrow, and if tomorrow matters enough, the threat of that punishment holds cooperation in place. The turning point is a single number.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Defecting still pays more this round: 5 beats 3. What changed is the shadow of the future. Against a grim-trigger opponent, one defection ends cooperation for good, trading a stream of 3s for a stream of 1s. When the future is heavy enough, that lost stream costs more than the one-time gain of 2, so you cooperate. When the future is light, the quick 2 wins and cooperation collapses. The whole thing turns on how much tomorrow is worth.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="discounted repeated game">
The stage game is repeated, and a player values a payoff $t$ rounds from now at $\delta^t$ times its face value, where $\delta \in (0,1)$ is the discount factor. Equivalently, after each round the game continues with probability $\delta$. A strategy is a rule that maps the whole history so far to a move.
</DefinitionAside>

Consider **grim trigger**: cooperate until the opponent defects once, then defect forever. Suppose both play it. Cooperating forever is worth

$$
R + \delta R + \delta^2 R + \cdots = \frac{R}{1-\delta}.
$$

Deviating means defecting once for $T$, after which the opponent punishes and you both get $P$ forever:

$$
T + \delta P + \delta^2 P + \cdots = T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta}.
$$

Cooperation is a best response, so grim trigger is a subgame-perfect equilibrium, exactly when the first is at least the second:

$$
\frac{R}{1-\delta} \ \ge\ T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta \ \ge\ \frac{T-R}{T-P}.
$$

With $T=5, R=3, P=1$ the threshold is $\tfrac{5-3}{5-1} = \tfrac12$. Value the future at least as much as the present-by-half, and cooperation stands; value it less, and it falls.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Derive the threshold, then move the numbers">
Set cooperating forever, $R/(1-\delta)$, against defecting once and taking the punishment, $T + \delta P/(1-\delta)$. Multiply through by $1-\delta$:

$$
R \ \ge\ T(1-\delta) + \delta P.
$$

Rearranging, $R - T \ge -\delta(T - P)$, so $\delta \ge (T-R)/(T-P)$. For $T=5, R=3, P=1$ this is $\tfrac12$.

**Faded.** Suppose mutual defection were less bad, $P=2$. The threshold becomes $(5-3)/(5-2) = \tfrac23$: a softer punishment means you need more patience to deter cheating.

**Solo.** At $\delta = 0.6$, does tit-for-tat sustain cooperation? Since $0.6 \ge \tfrac12$, yes. The same threshold works, because a single defection against tit-for-tat also costs you the cooperative stream.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Repetition does not make cooperation automatic. It makes it possible, and only when players value the future enough: here, when the game continues with probability at least one-half.</Callout>
</Section>
