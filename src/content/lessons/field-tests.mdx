# Field tests and boundaries

<p class="lead">Behavioral game theory is not "people are irrational." It is a map of where the sharp theory holds and where it breaks. Zero-sum minimax survives its hardest test among elite athletes under pressure. Backward induction in the centipede fails outright. Knowing which is which is the point.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The two field results pull in opposite directions. Penalty kicks are a clean zero-sum game played by experts with huge stakes and endless practice, and there minimax holds: kickers and keepers mix at the right frequencies with no pattern. The centipede is different: mutual passing makes both far better off, and players, trusting a little or valuing the larger pie, pass rather than grab. Backward induction's relentless unraveling assumes a level of mutual distrust that real, repeated human interaction does not have.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="two field verdicts">
Palacios-Huerta (2003) analysed 1,417 professional penalty kicks: scoring rates make the kicker's equilibrium mix about 36 percent to one side, and observed play matches the minimax frequencies with no serial correlation. Minimax passes its field test. McKelvey and Palfrey (1992) ran the centipede in the lab: subgame-perfect play says take at the first node, yet only about 1 percent do, and most pass several times. Backward induction fails its field test.
</DefinitionAside>

Fold the centipede backward: at the last node the mover takes ($5 > 4$ for Them), so the previous mover takes ($4 > 3$ for You), and so on to the first node, where You take for $(2,0)$. That is the subgame-perfect prediction. But mutual passing reaches $(6,4)$, which both prefer to $(2,0)$. Real players capture much of that surplus by passing, contradicting the fold. The penalty-kick result, by contrast, confirms the theory exactly where it should be hardest: strategic, high-pressure, expert play.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Fold the centipede, then compare to the field">
Solve backward. Last node (Them): Take gives Them $5$, Pass gives $4$, so Take, outcome $(3,5)$. Prior node (You): Take gives You $4$, passing leads to $(3,5)$ where You get $3$, so Take, outcome $(4,2)$. Next (Them): Take gives $3$, passing leads to $(4,2)$ giving Them $2$, so Take. First node (You): Take gives $2$, passing leads to Them taking for You's $1$, so Take. Backward induction: take immediately, $(2,0)$.

Now the field. In the centipede, real pairs pass repeatedly, often reaching the cooperative end near $(6,4)$, far better for both than $(2,0)$. The theory mispredicts. But run the other field test, penalty kicks, and professionals mix at the minimax frequencies with no readable pattern, confirming the theory. Same discipline, opposite verdicts, which is exactly the boundary this lesson maps.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">The sharp theory has boundaries. Zero-sum minimax holds among elite athletes under pressure; backward induction fails in the centipede, where real players pass to reach outcomes both prefer.</Callout>
</Section>
