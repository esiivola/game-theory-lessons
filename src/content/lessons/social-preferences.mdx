# Social preferences: fairness

<p class="lead">The subgame-perfect prediction for the ultimatum game is stark: offer a penny, and it will be accepted. Real responders reject lowball offers, and dictators give money away with nothing forcing them to. People care about fairness, and modeling that as part of the payoff explains the data.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Standard theory assumes the responder cares only about their own dollars, so any positive amount beats zero. But people also dislike being treated unfairly, and that distaste is real enough to outweigh a small payout. Rejecting a stingy offer is not irrational once you include fairness in the utility: the responder is trading a little money for the satisfaction of not being exploited. Proposers anticipate this, which is why real offers cluster near an even split, not at a penny.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="inequity aversion (Fehr-Schmidt)">
A responder who keeps $s$ (proposer keeps $\text{pie} - s$) has utility $s - \alpha\max(\text{other} - s, 0) - \beta\max(s - \text{other}, 0)$: the envy term $\alpha$ penalizes being behind, the guilt term $\beta$ (smaller) penalizes being ahead. Rejecting yields $0$, so the responder rejects any offer whose utility is negative. This turns fairness into part of the payoff, restoring rational choice.
</DefinitionAside>

When behind ($s < \text{pie}/2$), utility is $s - \alpha(\text{pie} - 2s)$, which is negative when $s < \dfrac{\alpha \cdot \text{pie}}{1 + 2\alpha}$. With $\alpha = 0.5$ and a pie of $10$, the threshold is $\dfrac{0.5 \cdot 10}{2} = 2.5$: the responder rejects offers below $\$2.50$, matching the empirical rejection spike. Dictator games (where the responder cannot reject) show smaller but still positive giving, and trust games show investors sending money that trustees partly return, all evidence that people value more than their own cash.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Find the rejection threshold">
Suppose the responder is offered $s$ with envy weight $\alpha = 0.5$ on a $\$10$ pie. Being behind, their utility is $s - 0.5\,(\,(10 - s) - s\,) = s - 0.5(10 - 2s) = s - 5 + s = 2s - 5$.

Set this to zero: $2s - 5 = 0$, so $s = 2.5$. Below $\$2.50$ the utility is negative, so the responder rejects, preferring the $0$ of walking away to the sting of a lopsided split. Above $\$2.50$ they accept. A stronger envy weight raises this threshold, and a proposer who understands it offers close to half to avoid rejection, exactly what the experiments show.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">People reject unfair offers and give unprompted, so payoffs are utility, not just money. Modeling fairness as inequity aversion turns "irrational" rejections into rational choices and predicts near-even offers.</Callout>
</Section>
