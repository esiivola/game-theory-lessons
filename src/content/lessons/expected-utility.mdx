# Preferences and expected utility

<p class="lead">To compare risky options we need utilities, not just money. A rational player maximises expected <span class="in-acc">utility</span>, and a curved utility can make a sure thing beat a better-on-average gamble.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The two outcomes are equally likely, but they are not equally weighted in utility. Going from \$50 down to \$0 costs more happiness than going from \$50 up to \$100 adds, because each extra dollar is worth a little less than the last. So the gamble's expected utility sits below the utility of its \$50 average, and the sure thing that matches it, the certainty equivalent, is less than \$50.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="eu">
## Value the risk, not the cash

Assign each amount a utility $u(x)$. The value of a gamble is its expected utility, the probability-weighted average of $u$. If $u$ is concave (each extra dollar is worth a little less than the last), the player is risk-averse and will pay to avoid a fair bet. That is exactly why insurance exists.

<DefinitionAside label="expected utility">
The von Neumann and Morgenstern theorem says a player whose preferences over lotteries obey a few consistency axioms acts as if maximising $\mathbb{E}[u(x)]$ for some utility $u$, unique up to a positive affine transformation.
</DefinitionAside>

<WorkedExample title="Find the certainty equivalent">
Take $u(x)=\sqrt{x}$ and the gamble (50% of \$0, 50% of \$100). Its expected utility is $0.5\sqrt{0}+0.5\sqrt{100}=5$. The sure amount worth the same is $x$ with $\sqrt{x}=5$, so $x=\$25$. This player would swap the gamble for a guaranteed \$25, well below its \$50 average. That gap is the risk premium.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Rational does not mean chasing the highest average payout. It means maximising expected utility, which can be cautious by design.</Callout>
</Section>
