# Public goods and the commons

<p class="lead">A public good is one everyone shares whether or not they paid for it: clean air, a stocked fishery, a stable climate. That sharing is the problem. When the benefit of your contribution spreads to everyone, your own slice of it is too small to justify the cost, so the sensible move is to let others carry it.</p>

## Predict, then play

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Put in a dollar and the pot grows by \$1.60, but that \$1.60 is split four ways, so you personally get 40 cents back on your dollar: a loss of 60 cents. That is true no matter what anyone else does, so contributing nothing is a dominant strategy. Yet the same arithmetic run for the group says every dollar contributed creates \$1.60 of value. Private incentive and group interest point in opposite directions, and watching the cooperators give up round by round is the commons collapsing in slow motion.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Four players, each endowed with \$10. Let $g_i$ be $i$'s contribution. The pot $\sum_j g_j$ is multiplied by $1.6$ and split evenly, so

$$
u_i = 10 - g_i + \frac{1.6}{4}\sum_j g_j = 10 - g_i + 0.4\sum_j g_j.
$$

The **marginal per-capita return** is $0.4$: each dollar you contribute returns $0.40$ to you. Since $0.4 < 1$, raising $g_i$ lowers $u_i$ whatever the others do, so $g_i = 0$ is a dominant strategy and the unique Nash equilibrium is no contribution at all.

Now the group. Total welfare is $\sum_i u_i = 40 + (1.6 - 1)\sum_j g_j = 40 + 0.6\sum_j g_j$, which rises in every contribution. So the social optimum is full contribution, \$10 each, and everyone ends with \$16 rather than \$10.

<DefinitionAside label="public-goods game">
A public good is non-excludable: its benefit reaches everyone regardless of who paid. When the private return to contributing (here $0.4$) is below $1$ but the social return (here $1.6$) is above $1$, self-interest gives zero and efficiency demands full contribution. That gap is the tragedy of the commons.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="reality">
## A note from the real world

<Callout kind="reality-check">
Theory predicts zero contribution. Real people do better than that. In public-goods experiments, most players start out giving around half, and many are conditional cooperators: they contribute if others do. But without a way to punish free-riders, contributions decay toward zero over repeated rounds, just as in the play above. The Behavioral unit takes up why cooperation starts high, why it unravels, and what sustains it.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Private loss, social gain">
Contribute \$1. The pot grows by \$1.60, split four ways, so you get \$0.40 back. Netting the dollar you put in, you are down \$0.60. Because this holds regardless of the others, the self-interested contribution is \$0.

Now suppose all four contribute \$1. The pot grows by \$6.40, and each person's share is \$1.60. Netting each person's own dollar, everyone is up \$0.60. The move that is privately bad is collectively good, which is why the equilibrium (all zero) sits so far below the optimum (all ten).
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When a benefit is shared but the cost is private, contributing nothing is each person's dominant move, and the group ends up far poorer than it could be. The commons fails not from malice but from ordinary self-interest.</Callout>
</Section>
