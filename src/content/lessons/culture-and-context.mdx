# Culture, context, and design

<p class="lead">The fairness this unit measured is not a universal constant. Cross-cultural experiments show offers and rejections vary widely with how a society is organized, and undergraduate subjects turn out to be outliers. A mechanism that assumes one fairness norm can fail badly on players who hold another.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
People bring the norms of their daily lives into the lab. Societies with lots of anonymous market exchange have strong norms of fair dealing with strangers, so they offer close to half and punish stinginess. Groups that rarely trade with outsiders may see a stranger's money as a windfall, offer little, and rarely reject. A few societies with heavy gift-giving norms even make over-generous offers that get rejected because accepting would incur an obligation. The "fairness" a designer counts on is a local product, not a human universal.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="WEIRD subjects and market integration">
Most experimental results come from WEIRD populations (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic), which turn out to be unusual. Henrich and colleagues ran the ultimatum game in fifteen small-scale societies: mean offers ranged from about 26 percent (the Machiguenga) to 57 percent (the Lamalera whale hunters), and rejection behaviour varied with the group's degree of market integration and cooperation in daily life. Fairness norms are learned from institutions, not fixed by biology.
</DefinitionAside>

In inequity-aversion terms, the envy weight $\alpha$ that governs when a responder rejects differs across cultures: higher in market-integrated groups (they punish unfairness), lower in insular ones (they accept low offers). A designer who plugs in a single $\alpha$, typically the WEIRD value, will mispredict behaviour elsewhere. The practical upshot connects back to market design: the pre-1998 medical match and Boston's old school mechanism failed partly because they assumed behaviour their participants did not exhibit. Good mechanisms are built for the players you actually have.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="One game, three thresholds">
Take the same $\$10$ ultimatum and vary the responder's envy weight $\alpha$. A market-integrated responder with high $\alpha$ rejects sizable offers below an even split, so a proposer must offer close to half. A WEIRD responder ($\alpha$ around $0.5$) rejects below roughly a quarter. A small-scale-society responder with low $\alpha$ accepts almost any positive offer, so a proposer can offer little and still succeed.

A designer optimizing offers, or building a market that relies on fair play, gets very different answers depending on which $\alpha$ holds. Assume the WEIRD value everywhere and you will over- or under-shoot elsewhere. The lesson: measure the population's actual norms, and design the mechanism for them, not for an idealized universal player.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Fairness is learned from institutions and varies across cultures, and the usual lab subjects are outliers. Effective mechanism design measures the norms of the actual players rather than assuming a universal one.</Callout>
</Section>
