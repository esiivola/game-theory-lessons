# Quantal response equilibrium

<p class="lead">Level-k relaxed how deeply players reason. Quantal response relaxes how sharply they act. Instead of always best-responding, players pick better actions with higher probability, and equilibrium is where these noisy choices are mutually consistent. A single rationality parameter tunes it from random to Nash.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
People are payoff-sensitive but noisy: a better option is more attractive, so it is chosen more, but small mistakes remain. The rationality parameter measures how sensitive: at zero, payoffs are ignored and choice is a coin flip; as it grows, the better action dominates, and in the limit you recover exact best response. Crucially, beliefs stay consistent, each player's noisy choices are a best noisy-response to the others', so this is still an equilibrium, just a smoothed one.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="quantal response equilibrium">
In a logit QRE, a player chooses action $a$ with probability proportional to $\exp(\lambda \cdot EU(a))$, where $EU(a)$ is its expected payoff and $\lambda$ is the rationality (precision) parameter. Equilibrium requires these choice probabilities to be mutually consistent. At $\lambda = 0$ all actions are equally likely; as $\lambda \to \infty$ the distribution concentrates on best responses, recovering Nash.
</DefinitionAside>

For two actions with expected-payoff difference $d = EU(A) - EU(B)$, the probability of $A$ is

$$
P(A) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\lambda d}}.
$$

With $d = 1$: $\lambda = 0$ gives $0.50$, $\lambda = 1$ gives about $0.73$, $\lambda = 3$ gives about $0.95$, and $\lambda \to \infty$ gives $1$. Fitting $\lambda$ to experimental data typically lands at a moderate value, which lets QRE explain systematic deviations from Nash, like overbidding in auctions or turnout in voting, that sharp best response cannot.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Turn the dial from noise to Nash">
Take a one-util advantage, $d = 1$, and read off $P(A) = 1/(1 + e^{-\lambda})$. At $\lambda = 0$, $P(A) = 1/2$: payoffs are ignored entirely. At $\lambda = 1$, $P(A) = 1/(1 + e^{-1}) \approx 0.73$: the better action is favoured but far from certain. At $\lambda = 3$, $P(A) \approx 0.95$, and as $\lambda$ grows without bound, $P(A) \to 1$, the pure best response.

So QRE is a one-parameter family spanning uniform randomization to Nash. Experimental play typically sits at an intermediate $\lambda$, which is why QRE can match graded, mistake-prone data while still being an equilibrium concept with consistent beliefs.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Quantal response equilibrium replaces perfect best response with noisy, payoff-sensitive choice governed by one rationality dial. It spans random play to Nash and fits real, mistake-prone behaviour in between.</Callout>
</Section>
