# Classic 2x2 games

<p class="lead">The Prisoner's Dilemma is one animal in a small zoo. Change the four payoffs and you get coordination games, games of nerve, and games with no stable move at all. Learning the family is worth more than memorizing any one game, because the structure tells you what to look for.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The four numbers are the whole story. Where the temptation to deviate sits, and whether players want to match or to differ, decides everything: whether there is a dominant move, how many stable cells there are, and whether the stable cells make players agree or fight over which one to reach. Nudge one payoff past a threshold and a coordination game can turn into a dilemma. The families are regions in payoff space, not separate universes.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Five games map the territory.

<DefinitionAside label="the five families">
**Prisoner's Dilemma** (R3, S0, T5, P1): defecting dominates, so the unique Nash is mutual defection, worse for both than cooperating.
**Stag Hunt** ((4,4)/(0,3)/(3,0)/(3,3)): two Nash on the diagonal. All-Stag pays more (payoff-dominant), but Hare is safer against an uncertain partner (risk-dominant).
**Battle of the Sexes** ((2,1)/(0,0)/(0,0)/(1,2)): two Nash, but each player prefers a different one. Coordination with conflict.
**Chicken** ((6,6)/(2,7)/(7,2)/(0,0)): two Nash off the diagonal. Each wants to do the opposite of the other, and the crash is worst for both.
**Matching Pennies** ((1,-1)/(-1,1)/(-1,1)/(1,-1)): one wants to match, the other to differ. No pure Nash exists.
</DefinitionAside>

Three lessons hide in this list. Not every dilemma is a Prisoner's Dilemma: Stag Hunt has a fully cooperative equilibrium the PD lacks, and in Chicken, where the equilibria are off-diagonal, backing down is at least a best reply to aggression rather than a mistake. Two equilibria does not mean the players are indifferent: in Battle of the Sexes they actively disagree about which to pick. And no pure Nash does not mean unsolvable: Matching Pennies has a mixed equilibrium, which is where the next lesson goes.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Classify Stag Hunt, then Matching Pennies">
**Stag Hunt.** Mark best responses. If your partner hunts Stag, your best reply is Stag (4 > 3); if they take the Hare, your best reply is Hare (3 > 0). So both (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare) are Nash. Both players prefer (Stag, Stag) at $(4,4)$, yet Hare guarantees $3$ no matter what the partner does, while Stag risks $0$. That safety is why groups can get stuck at the worse equilibrium.

**Matching Pennies.** Mark best responses again. Whatever cell you point at, one player always wants to switch: the matcher chases, the mismatcher flees. The best-response marks never coincide, so there is no pure Nash. The only stable way to play is to randomize, which is the mixed strategy of the next lesson.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">The same 2x2 shape holds a family of games. Read the payoffs for where the temptation sits and whether players want to match, and the equilibrium structure, one, two, or none, follows.</Callout>
</Section>
