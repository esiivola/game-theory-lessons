# The commons

<p class="lead">A common-pool resource, such as a fishery or pasture, is rival in use but hard to exclude people from. Private incentives can produce overuse, but collapse is not inevitable. Ostrom documented communities governing shared resources with locally fitted rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each user receives the full catch but bears only part of the future depletion cost. When total harvest exceeds biological growth, the stock falls. Continued overharvest can then reduce future growth as well as future catch. Monitoring and sanctions may change extraction incentives, but they do not automatically guarantee restraint. The rule, detection probability, penalty, and users' continuation incentives all matter.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real evidence

<DefinitionAside label="Ostrom's design principles">
Elinor Ostrom's Governing the Commons (1990) examined long-lasting common-pool resources, including pastures, irrigation systems, forests, and fisheries, and described eight recurring design principles. Two are monitoring and graduated sanctions: first offences draw relatively small penalties, while repeat offences draw larger ones. These institutions can reduce the payoff from overuse, but the equilibrium still depends on detection, sanctions, resource dynamics, and expectations about future play.
</DefinitionAside>

The game now uses a stock $S_t$ with carrying capacity $K$ and logistic growth rate $r$:

$$S_{t+1}=\max\!\left\{0,\min\!\left[K,S_t+rS_t\left(1-\frac{S_t}{K}\right)-H_t\right]\right\},$$

where $H_t$ is total harvest. Growth is largest at $S_t=K/2$, where the maximum sustainable yield is $rK/4$. A catch below current growth raises the stock; a catch above current growth lowers it. A graduated sanction adds a penalty that rises with the amount over the rule and with repeated violations. Whether that deters overharvest depends on expected detection and future consequences.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="From collapse to sustainable yield">
With a stock of $60$, $K=100$, and $r=0.8$, biological growth is $0.8(60)(1-60/100)=19.2$. If total catch is $20$, the next stock is $59.2$. If total catch is $16$, it rises to $63.2$. The sustainable catch is therefore state-dependent, not a fixed contribution to a pot.

Now add a catch limit of $4$, monitoring, and a penalty that rises after each repeated violation. In the demonstration, the high extractor responds by reducing catch. That response is an assumption you can see, not a theorem. Ostrom's evidence shows that monitoring and graduated sanctions often appear in durable self-governed commons. It does not establish that one penalty schedule always beats every alternative.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">A commons is a stock-flow problem: today’s private harvest changes tomorrow’s shared resource. Monitoring and graduated sanctions can support restraint, but their effect depends on the biological dynamics and the incentives they create.</Callout>
</Section>
