# Perfect Bayesian equilibrium

<p class="lead">When players move in turn and some of them hide private information, equilibrium needs two things at once: a strategy for everyone, and a belief at every point about what has happened so far. Perfect Bayesian equilibrium ties them together, so each move is optimal given beliefs, and beliefs are updated from the moves.</p>

## Predict, then play

The Gift game. A sender is a Friend (good intentions) with probability $p$, or an Enemy otherwise, and chooses whether to give you a gift. Seeing a gift but not the type, you accept or reject. Accepting a Friend's gift is good, an Enemy's is bad. Set the prior and check whether "both types give" can hold up.

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Your decision hinges on a belief: how likely is this gift to come from a Friend? If both types give, the gift is uninformative, so your belief stays at the prior. When the prior is high enough you accept, and giving pays off for both types, so the arrangement holds. When the prior is too low you reject, and a gift becomes a trap you avoid, so no one bothers to give. The equilibrium is a strategy and a belief that agree with each other.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="perfect Bayesian equilibrium">
A perfect Bayesian equilibrium is a strategy profile together with a belief at every information set such that: (1) beliefs on the equilibrium path are derived from the strategies by Bayes' rule; (2) every player acts sequentially rationally, choosing the best action given their belief at each information set, on and off the path; and (3) off-path beliefs are unrestricted by Bayes but the actions they induce must still be optimal. It extends subgame perfection to games where private information means proper subgames may not exist.
</DefinitionAside>

In the Gift game the receiver's belief after a gift is $b = \Pr(\text{Friend} \mid \text{gift})$. Accepting is worth $b\cdot 1 + (1-b)\cdot(-1) = 2b - 1$, and rejecting is worth $0$, so the receiver accepts iff $b \ge \tfrac12$.

Consider both types giving. On the path Bayes sets $b = p$, the prior. If $p \ge \tfrac12$ the receiver accepts, and since both senders prefer a gift accepted (payoff 1) to not giving (0), pooling on Give is a PBE. If $p < \tfrac12$ the receiver rejects, both senders then earn $-1 < 0$ and deviate to Not, so pooling on Give fails; the PBE is pooling on Not, supported by an off-path belief that a gift would come from an Enemy.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Two priors, two equilibria">
**p = 0.6.** Suppose both types give. On the path a gift implies belief $0.6$, and accepting is worth $2(0.6) - 1 = 0.2 > 0$, so the receiver accepts. Both senders then get $1$ from giving versus $0$ from not, so neither deviates. Pooling on Give is a PBE.

**p = 0.4.** Suppose both types give. Now a gift implies belief $0.4$, accepting is worth $-0.2 < 0$, so the receiver rejects, and both senders get $-1 < 0$: they deviate to Not. The surviving PBE is pooling on Not. To support it, assign the off-path belief after a (never-sent) gift to be Enemy, so a deviating giver would be rejected and earn $-1$, confirming that not giving is optimal.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">A perfect Bayesian equilibrium pairs strategies with beliefs: act optimally given your belief at every point, and update beliefs by Bayes wherever you can. A signal is trusted only when the belief it induces clears the bar for acting on it.</Callout>
</Section>
