# Do professionals really play minimax?

<p class="lead">Most predictions of game theory are hard to test, because you cannot see the payoffs or count the plays. Sport is the exception: thousands of penalties and serves, recorded, with the scoring rates visible. The verdict is unusually kind to the theory on frequencies, and slightly less kind on randomness.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Minimax makes two separate claims, and the data treat them differently. The first is about frequencies: play each option at the rate that equalizes the opponent's options. That claim survives well. Success rates by side come out statistically equal, which is exactly what the equilibrium requires, and the observed frequencies sit close to the predicted ones. The second claim is about randomness: successive choices should be independent, with no readable pattern. Here the record is more mixed. Tennis servers switch sides slightly too often, a mild negative correlation, which is the classic human failure to produce a random sequence. It is a small effect, and an opponent would need a lot of data to profit from it, but it is there.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real evidence

<DefinitionAside label="what a field test of minimax has to check">
Equilibrium in these games implies two testable restrictions. **Equal success rates:** if a player mixes over several options, every option in the mix must yield the same expected payoff, so scoring rates by side should be statistically indistinguishable. **Serial independence:** the choice on one attempt should carry no information about the next, so the sequence should show no correlation. Both are sharp, and a violation of either is money the opponent can take.
</DefinitionAside>

Three studies, three verdicts. Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002) analysed professional penalties and could not reject equal scoring rates across sides, with frequencies in the predicted range. Palacios-Huerta (2003) added a large sample of European kicks and found the observed mixing frequencies close to the computed equilibrium ones, and no serial correlation he could detect. Walker and Wooders (2001) ran the same two tests on the direction of serve at Wimbledon: winning rates by serve direction came out equal, as the theory demands, but servers alternated sides slightly more than chance would produce, a small but statistically real departure from independence.

So the frequency prediction holds up across sports, and the randomness prediction very nearly does. That pattern, sharp on the payoff-equalizing condition and slightly soft on true randomness, is about as good as an economic theory tested on human beings gets.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Run the two tests yourself">
**Test one, equal success rates.** Take a player's record and split it by side: goals per shot to the left, goals per shot to the right. Equilibrium says these two numbers should be equal, because a player who mixes must be indifferent. If one side scores reliably better, the player is under-using it and leaving goals on the table. Across the professional samples the two rates come out statistically indistinguishable, which is the theory's cleanest win.

**Test two, serial independence.** Now ignore the rates and look only at the sequence of sides. Count how often a choice differs from the one before. Under true randomness that should be about half the time. Wimbledon servers switch a little more often than that, the familiar human habit of avoiding repeats to feel random. The bias is small, so it takes many observations to exploit, but a keeper or returner with enough tape could.

Play the shootout above and both failures show up in the meter: lean on one side and the keeper's read climbs, or alternate tidily and it climbs anyway. Passing the first test is not enough; you have to pass the second at the same time.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Minimax makes two testable claims, and sport lets you check both. Professionals pass the equal-success-rate test convincingly, and come close on serial independence, missing mainly by switching sides a little too often.</Callout>
</Section>
