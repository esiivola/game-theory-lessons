# Mixed strategies in sport

<p class="lead">Minimax is not just a theorem; it is on the field. Penalty kicks, tennis serves, and pitch selection are zero-sum guessing games where any predictable pattern gets punished. The equilibrium is to mix at the frequencies that make the opponent indifferent, and that is usually not 50/50.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The kicker wants to score; the keeper wants to save; one's gain is the other's loss, so this is zero-sum. If you favour your strong side too much, an adaptive keeper dives that way more often and cuts your success. If you kick purely 50/50, you underuse your more accurate side. The minimax mix sits exactly where your expected scoring is the same whichever way the keeper guesses, so the keeper has no way to raise the save rate. That frequency is not 50/50, because the two sides are not equally good; it tilts toward the stronger side but stays unpredictable. Empirical studies of professional kickers find their actual frequencies strikingly close to this prediction.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real evidence

<DefinitionAside label="minimax on the pitch">
In the penalty game the kicker mixes over sides and the keeper mixes over dives; success rates differ by side (kicking to your natural side is more accurate, but the keeper knows that too). The equilibrium mix makes the kicker's scoring equal across the keeper's responses, so neither can improve unilaterally. Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002) and Palacios-Huerta (2003) tested this on thousands of real penalties and found professionals mix at close to the equilibrium frequencies, and serially independently (no detectable pattern), exactly as minimax requires.
</DefinitionAside>

The point is not "randomize blindly" but "randomize at the right rate". Because the natural side is more accurate, the equilibrium leans toward it, roughly a 36 to 64 split rather than 50/50 in the standard scoring, but every side must be played often enough that the keeper cannot profitably favour one dive. Any drift from the equilibrium frequency, or any exploitable pattern (alternating, streaks), lets an adaptive opponent raise their save rate. The remarkable empirical finding is how well top professionals approximate both the right frequencies and genuine serial independence.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why the mix is not 50/50, and why patterns lose">
Suppose kicking to your natural side scores more often when the keeper guesses wrong, but the keeper, knowing this, is tempted to dive that way. To keep the keeper indifferent, you play your natural side often enough that diving there is no better than diving the other way. Because the sides differ in accuracy, that indifference point is not 50/50; with the standard scoring the kicker goes to one side about 36% of the time. At that mix, the keeper cannot raise the save rate by favouring either dive.

Now break the mix. Always take your strong side: the keeper simply commits to that dive and your scoring collapses. Alternate sides to feel random: alternation is a pattern, and an adaptive keeper reads it and anticipates each kick. Even a slight bias, played repeatedly, hands the keeper an edge. That is why the exploitability meter climbs whenever your play departs from equilibrium frequencies or serial independence, and why the striking empirical result is how close professionals stay to both.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">In zero-sum sport, the winning strategy is to mix at the exact frequencies that make your opponent indifferent, usually not 50/50, and with no detectable pattern. Real professionals play remarkably close to this minimax prediction; any bias or pattern is exploitable.</Callout>
</Section>
