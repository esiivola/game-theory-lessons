# Repeated games with imperfect monitoring

<p class="lead">Grim trigger assumes you can tell when someone cheated. Often you cannot: you see a noisy signal that reflects both the other player's action and pure chance. Then punishment can no longer be reserved for real defections, and keeping cooperation alive means accepting that innocent players sometimes get punished too.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
With noise, a bad signal does not prove anyone cheated. But if you never punish a bad signal, cheating becomes free, so some punishment must follow bad signals regardless of the cause. That means wars will sometimes strike honest players. Grim trigger takes this to the extreme: the first unlucky signal ends everything. The fix is to make punishment finite. You accept that occasional wars are the price of policing a deal you cannot see, and you forgive so cooperation can resume.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Suppose that in any cooperative round the public signal reads "bad" with probability $q$, even when both cooperated (a false alarm), and worse if someone defected. Under **grim trigger** (punish forever after one bad signal), the relationship survives only until the first false alarm. Since a false alarm arrives with probability $q$ each round, it is essentially certain eventually, so cooperation collapses to zero.

A **trigger-and-forgive** strategy punishes for $T$ rounds after a bad signal, then resets. The long-run fraction of time spent cooperating is

$$
\text{coop fraction} = \frac{1}{1 + qT},
$$

which is positive for finite $T$ and falls as noise $q$ or punishment length $T$ rise.

<DefinitionAside label="imperfect public monitoring (Green-Porter)">
Players observe only a public signal correlated with the profile of actions, not the actions themselves. On the equilibrium path, punishments must be triggered by signals, so they sometimes fire when no one deviated. Sustaining cooperation requires accepting these on-path punishment phases; the optimal ones are finite and forgiving, not permanent.
</DefinitionAside>

Observed price wars are therefore not proof of cheating. In the Green-Porter model, cartels endure recurring price wars that nobody chose, set off by low prices that only looked like betrayal.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why grim fails, and forgiveness saves value">
Let the false-alarm rate be $q = 0.1$. Under grim trigger, cooperation ends the first time the signal reads bad. The chance of surviving $n$ rounds is $0.9^n$, which decays toward zero, so cooperation almost surely collapses and stays collapsed. All the value is lost to a punishment that punishes no one in particular.

Now use trigger-and-forgive with $T = 4$. The cooperative fraction is $1/(1 + 0.1\times 4) = 1/1.4 \approx 0.71$. Wars still happen, about once every ten cooperative rounds, and last four rounds each, but the relationship recovers every time. A little forgiveness converts a doomed relationship into a mostly cooperative one.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When you can only see a noisy signal, punishment must sometimes fall on the innocent. Permanent punishment destroys the relationship; finite, forgiving punishment sustains cooperation with occasional wars no one caused.</Callout>
</Section>
