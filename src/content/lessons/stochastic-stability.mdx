# Stochastic stability and conventions

<p class="lead">A coordination game has several equilibria, and the ESS cannot say which a society lands on. Add rare mistakes, mutations or experiments, and the tie breaks: the population spends almost all its time at the risk-dominant convention, the one with the larger basin, regardless of which pays more.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Both all-Stag and all-Hare are stable against a single defector. What separates them is how much noise it takes to escape each. Escaping all-Hare toward Stag requires a lot of players to mistakenly hunt at once, because Stag only pays when most others do. Escaping all-Stag toward Hare needs only a few mistakes, since Hare is safe. So random shocks tip the population out of Stag easily and out of Hare rarely, and over a long horizon it lingers at Hare, even though everyone would prefer Stag.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="stochastic stability and risk-dominance">
In an evolutionary model where players best-respond but occasionally make random mistakes (mutations), a convention is stochastically stable if the population spends a non-vanishing fraction of time there as the mistake rate goes to zero. For 2x2 coordination games it is the risk-dominant equilibrium, the one whose basin of attraction is larger, that is stochastically stable. Payoff-dominance does not decide it.
</DefinitionAside>

In the Stag Hunt, Stag pays $4$ against Stag and $0$ against Hare; Hare pays $3$ regardless. Playing Stag is a best response only if you believe the partner hunts with probability $q$ satisfying $4q \ge 3$, that is $q \ge \tfrac34$. So Stag's basin (the beliefs for which it is optimal) is only $\tfrac14$, while Hare's is $\tfrac34$.

Since Hare's basin exceeds one-half, Hare is risk-dominant and therefore stochastically stable. Lowering the mutation rate does not change which convention wins; it only lengthens the time the population waits before drifting back to Hare. The long-run outcome is start-independent.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Compare the basins">
You should hunt Stag only if the expected payoff beats Hare. Stag pays $4q$ when the partner hunts with probability $q$ (and $0$ otherwise); Hare pays $3$ no matter what. So Stag is a best response iff $4q \ge 3$, i.e., $q \ge \tfrac34$. That leaves only a $\tfrac14$ slice of beliefs favouring Stag, and $\tfrac34$ favouring Hare.

The larger basin belongs to Hare, so Hare is risk-dominant. In the noisy dynamics, the population is pushed out of the small Stag basin by even modest mutation, but escaping the large Hare basin requires an improbable coincidence of mistakes. So it settles at Hare in the long run, despite Stag being payoff-dominant. Lowering the mutation rate only makes the rare escapes rarer; it does not favour Stag.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">With rare mistakes, the long-run winner among conventions is the risk-dominant one, with the larger basin, not the payoff-dominant one. Lowering the noise changes only the waiting time, not the outcome.</Callout>
</Section>
