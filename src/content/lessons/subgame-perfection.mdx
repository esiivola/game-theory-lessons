# Subgame perfection and commitment

<p class="lead">Some Nash equilibria are held up by threats no one would carry out. Subgame perfection throws those away and keeps only the credible ones. The same idea, run forward, explains why moving first and committing, giving up your own flexibility, can leave you better off.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
When both firms move together, each hedges against the other. When you commit first, the rival can no longer hope you will back down: your large quantity is a fact, and its best reply is to make less. Because your commitment is visible and irreversible, it changes what the rival does. The same logic that exposes an empty threat, looking ahead to what each player would really do, is what makes a credible commitment valuable.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Run the entry game from the last lesson as a simultaneous table instead of a tree. It has two Nash equilibria. Show the best responses.

<PayoffMatrix
  client:visible
  mode="nash"
  exhibit="Exhibit 2"
  caption="The entry game in normal form (entrant, incumbent)"
  rows={[{ key: 'In', label: 'Enter', tone: 'coop' }, { key: 'Out', label: 'Stay out', tone: 'neutral' }]}
  cols={[{ key: 'A', label: 'Accommodate', tone: 'coop' }, { key: 'F', label: 'Fight', tone: 'def' }]}
  payoffs={{ In: { A: [2, 1], F: [-1, 0] }, Out: { A: [0, 2], F: [0, 2] } }}
/>

Two gold cells: $(\text{In},\text{Accommodate})$ and $(\text{Out},\text{Fight})$. Both are Nash. But $(\text{Out},\text{Fight})$ survives only because the incumbent's threat to fight is never tested when the entrant stays out.

<DefinitionAside label="subgame-perfect equilibrium">
A strategy profile is subgame perfect if it is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, not just in the game as a whole. Equivalently, it prescribes optimal play at every decision node, which is exactly what backward induction finds. Here only $(\text{In},\text{Accommodate})$ qualifies; the fight is not optimal in the subgame that follows entry.
</DefinitionAside>

Now commitment, through Stackelberg. The leader picks $q_1$, foreseeing the follower's reaction $q_2 = (120 - q_1)/2$. Substituting into the leader's profit,

$$
\pi_1 = \left(120 - q_1 - \tfrac{120 - q_1}{2}\right)q_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}(120 - q_1)\,q_1,
$$

which is maximized at $q_1 = 60$. Then $q_2 = 30$, $P = 30$, and the leader earns $1800$, above the Cournot $1600$. The ability to commit, not react, is worth $200$.
</Section>

<Section id="reality">
## A note from the real world

<Callout kind="reality-check">
Credibility has limits that are not on the blackboard. In the ultimatum game one player offers a split of \$10 and the other accepts or rejects; reject and both get nothing. Subgame perfection says offer a single penny and it will be taken, since a penny beats zero. Real responders routinely reject anything they see as unfair, walking away from real money to punish a lopsided offer. The Behavioral unit takes up why, and what it means for the theory of credible commitment.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Prune the empty threat, then solve Stackelberg">
**Credibility.** In the table, $(\text{Out},\text{Fight})$ is a Nash equilibrium: given the incumbent would fight, staying out is the entrant's best reply, and given the entrant stays out, the incumbent is indifferent, so fighting is a best reply too. But look at the subgame after entry: there, fighting pays $0$ and accommodating pays $1$. Fighting is not optimal in that subgame, so the profile is not subgame perfect. Only $(\text{In},\text{Accommodate})$ is.

**Commitment.** Substitute the follower's reaction $q_2 = (120 - q_1)/2$ into the leader's profit to get $\tfrac12(120 - q_1)q_1$. The first-order condition $60 - q_1 = 0$ gives $q_1 = 60$, then $q_2 = 30$, $P = 30$, leader profit $1800$. Moving first and committing beats the $1600$ of simultaneous Cournot, because the follower must adjust to a quantity it cannot undo.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Subgame perfection keeps only equilibria that are credible at every point in the game. Its forward-looking twin is commitment: a visible, irreversible move can beat keeping your options open.</Callout>
</Section>
