# Matching with contracts and design without money

<p class="lead">Many important markets forbid prices: school seats, organ transplants, cadet postings. Deferred acceptance carries over from the marriage market to school choice, where it gives stable assignments and makes honest ranking a dominant strategy for families. The same machinery extends to matching with contract terms.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Two students want school A, but A has one seat and a priority order, so it keeps the higher-priority student and rejects the other, who then tries their next choice. That rejection cascades: the displaced student bumps someone at the next school, and so on, until everyone is placed with no lingering blocking pair. Because a student never loses a seat by aiming high first, listing schools in true order is always safe. The Boston mechanism lacked this property, so families had to strategize about where to rank popular schools.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="school choice and matching with contracts">
Treat students as proposers and school priority orders as the receiving side's preferences. Student-proposing deferred acceptance yields a stable assignment (no student-school pair both prefer each other to their assignment) and is strategy-proof for students: truthful ranking is a dominant strategy. Matching with contracts (Hatfield-Milgrom) generalizes this: a "contract" bundles a partner with terms (a wage, a schedule), and under a substitutes condition a cumulative-offer process still produces a stable outcome, with auctions as a special case.
</DefinitionAside>

Run student-proposing DA. Round 1: $s_1 \to A$, $s_2 \to A$, $s_3 \to B$. School $A$ holds its higher priority $s_2$ and rejects $s_1$. Round 2: $s_1 \to B$; $B$ prefers $s_1$ to its current $s_3$, so it swaps, rejecting $s_3$. Round 3: $s_3 \to A$, rejected (A keeps $s_2$). Round 4: $s_3 \to C$, accepted. Final: $s_2 \to A$, $s_1 \to B$, $s_3 \to C$, a stable assignment.

Contrast with TTC, which would maximize efficiency but can violate priorities. School districts that value respecting priorities (stability) choose DA; the choice between DA and TTC is a real design trade-off, not a matter of one dominating the other.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Trace the rejection chain">
Students propose to their top schools: $s_1$ and $s_2$ both go to $A$, and $s_3$ goes to $B$. School $A$ ranks $s_2 > s_1$, so it tentatively holds $s_2$ and rejects $s_1$.

$s_1$ now proposes to its second choice, $B$, which already holds $s_3$. Since $B$ ranks $s_1 > s_3$, it swaps to $s_1$ and rejects $s_3$. Displaced, $s_3$ proposes to $A$, which keeps $s_2$ and rejects it; then $s_3$ proposes to $C$, which accepts. The chain settles at $s_2 \to A$, $s_1 \to B$, $s_3 \to C$. Every student ranked honestly, and none could have done better by lying, which is exactly the property the old Boston mechanism lacked.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Deferred acceptance turns school choice into a stable, strategy-proof assignment where families can safely rank honestly. It generalizes to matching with contract terms, and it trades off against efficiency-first mechanisms like top trading cycles.</Callout>
</Section>
