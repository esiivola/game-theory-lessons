# Signaling games

<p class="lead">When you know something an employer cannot verify, saying "I am talented" is cheap and unbelievable. What works is doing something costly that a less talented person would not find worthwhile. Spence showed education can play that role, sorting workers by type without teaching a thing.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The signal works because it is differentially costly. Education does not make anyone more productive here, but it hurts the Low worker more per year than the High worker. So there is a level of schooling the High type will pay for and the Low type will not, and a firm seeing that level can safely infer High. The diploma is not proof of skill; it is proof that faking it was not worth the trouble.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Low productivity is $\theta_L = 1$, High is $\theta_H = 2$, each with probability $\tfrac12$. Education $e$ adds nothing to productivity but costs $c(e,\theta) = e/\theta$. In a **separating equilibrium** the High type picks some $e^*$ and the Low type picks $0$; firms pay $w = 2$ for $e \ge e^*$ and $w = 1$ below.

Two incentive constraints must hold. The Low type must not want to mimic:

$$
\underbrace{2 - e^*}_{\text{mimic}} \le \underbrace{1}_{\text{stay at }0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^* \ge 1.
$$

The High type must prefer to separate:

$$
\underbrace{2 - \tfrac{e^*}{2}}_{\text{separate}} \ge \underbrace{1}_{\text{pool down}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^* \le 2.
$$

Any $e^*$ in $[1, 2]$ is a separating equilibrium, so there is a continuum. The **least-cost** one is $e^* = 1$: wages are $w(0) = 1$, $w(1) = 2$, and the High worker nets $2 - \tfrac12 = 1.5$.

<DefinitionAside label="single crossing and refinement">
The signal sorts types because the cost curves satisfy single crossing: the marginal cost of education is lower for the High type, so their indifference curves cross a wage-education line only once. That gap is what makes a separating level exist. The multiplicity of separating equilibria is usually resolved by a refinement (the intuitive criterion of Cho and Kreps), which selects the least-cost signal, and in the companion beer-quiche game rules out implausible pooling supported by odd off-path beliefs.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Find the least-cost separating education">
Write the two constraints. The Low type, if it buys education $e^*$ and is paid the High wage, nets $2 - e^*/1$. It will not mimic as long as that is no better than staying at zero for the Low wage: $2 - e^* \le 1$, so $e^* \ge 1$.

The High type buys $e^*$ at cost $e^*/2$ and is paid $2$, netting $2 - e^*/2$. It prefers this to skipping school for the Low wage of $1$ as long as $2 - e^*/2 \ge 1$, so $e^* \le 2$.

Both hold on $[1, 2]$. The cheapest signal that still separates is $e^* = 1$: the Low type is just indifferent about mimicking (so it does not), and the High type nets $2 - 0.5 = 1.5$. Signalling any more than $e^* = 1$ only burns money.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">A costly action credibly reveals private information when it is cheaper for one type than another. Education can sort workers by talent without adding any, and the efficient signal is the smallest one the weaker type would not copy.</Callout>
</Section>
