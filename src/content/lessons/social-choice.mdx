# Social choice: Arrow and Gibbard-Satterthwaite

<p class="lead">Turning individual rankings into a group ranking sounds like bookkeeping. It is not. Majority rule can cycle, and two theorems show the trouble is fundamental: no reasonable rule aggregates preferences without a dictator (Arrow), and none is immune to strategic voting (Gibbard-Satterthwaite).</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each individual has a perfectly sensible, transitive ranking. But majorities are made of different people in each pairwise contest, so the group's "preference" need not be consistent. There is no contradiction in any voter; the intransitivity is a property of aggregation itself. Once you see that majority rule can cycle, you start to suspect that no aggregation rule can be perfectly well-behaved, and Arrow proved exactly that.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Take rankings $A\!>\!B\!>\!C$, $B\!>\!C\!>\!A$, $C\!>\!A\!>\!B$. Pairwise: $A$ beats $B$ (voters 1 and 3), $B$ beats $C$ (voters 1 and 2), $C$ beats $A$ (voters 2 and 3). The social preference cycles, so there is no Condorcet winner.

<DefinitionAside label="Arrow and Gibbard-Satterthwaite">
Arrow's theorem: with three or more alternatives, no rule turning individual rankings into a social ranking can satisfy all of unrestricted domain, unanimity (Pareto), independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship at once. Gibbard-Satterthwaite: any onto voting rule over three or more alternatives that is strategy-proof (no voter ever gains by misreporting) must be a dictatorship. Together they say perfect aggregation and full strategy-proofness are impossible.
</DefinitionAside>

Strategic voting follows. Suppose plurality with $45\%$ for $A$, $30\%$ for $B$, $25\%$ for $C$, where the $C$-voters prefer $B$ to $A$. Voting sincerely elects $A$. But if the $C$-voters switch to $B$, then $B$ wins with $55\%$, an outcome they prefer. The rule is manipulable, exactly as Gibbard-Satterthwaite predicts.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="A cycle, then a manipulation">
**The cycle.** Count each pairwise contest in the profile above. $A$ vs $B$: voters 1 and 3 rank $A$ higher, so $A$ wins $2$ to $1$. $B$ vs $C$: voters 1 and 2 prefer $B$, so $B$ wins. $C$ vs $A$: voters 2 and 3 prefer $C$, so $C$ wins. The social preference is $A > B > C > A$: a cycle, no winner.

**The manipulation.** Under plurality with shares $45/30/25$ for $A/B/C$, sincere voting elects $A$. The $25\%$ who rank $C$ first but prefer $B$ to $A$ can desert $C$ and vote $B$. Now $B$ has $30 + 25 = 55\%$ and wins, which those voters prefer to $A$. A non-dictatorial rule was manipulated, illustrating Gibbard-Satterthwaite.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Aggregating preferences is not neutral bookkeeping. Majority rule can cycle, no rule escapes Arrow's conditions without a dictator, and none over three or more options is fully strategy-proof.</Callout>
</Section>
