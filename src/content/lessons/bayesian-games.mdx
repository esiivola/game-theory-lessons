# Bayesian games and Bayes-Nash

<p class="lead">Real strategic settings are full of things you do not know: a rival's cost, a bidder's value, a partner's preferences. Harsanyi's move was to treat that uncertainty as a draw of the other player's type from a known distribution, so you can best-respond to the odds even when you cannot see the outcome.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
You cannot see the rival's cost, so you cannot tailor your output to it. What you can do is form the expected rival output, averaging over the types, and best-respond to that. A low-cost rival produces more, dragging the price down, so the more likely they are low-cost, the more crowded the market you expect, and the less you should produce. You are best-responding to a distribution, not to a realized type.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="Bayesian game and Bayes-Nash equilibrium">
A Bayesian game adds a set of types for each player, a prior distribution over them, and payoffs that depend on the type profile. A strategy specifies an action for each of your own types. A Bayes-Nash equilibrium is a profile in which every type of every player is best-responding to the distribution of the others' types and their strategies.
</DefinitionAside>

Firm 2 of type with cost $c$ best-responds to $q_1$: maximizing $(12 - q_1 - q_2 - c)q_2$ gives

$$
q_2(c) = \frac{12 - q_1 - c}{2}.
$$

Firm 1 does not see $c$, so it best-responds to the expected rival output, taking that number as fixed: $q_1 = (12 - \mathbb{E}[q_2])/2$. The two conditions have to hold at once. With the rival low-cost ($c=0$) with probability $\mu$ and high-cost ($c=6$) otherwise, the type-by-type reactions give $\mathbb{E}[q_2] = 3 + 3\mu - q_1/2$, and substituting that into firm 1's best response solves the system:

$$
q_1 = \frac{12 - (3 + 3\mu - q_1/2)}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_1 = 6 - 2\mu.
$$

At $\mu = \tfrac12$, $q_1 = 5$, with $q_2(\text{low}) = 3.5$ and $q_2(\text{high}) = 0.5$. As $\mu \to 1$, $q_1 \to 4$: a rival more likely to be low-cost crowds the market, so firm 1 pulls back.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Solve the Bayes-Nash equilibrium">
Each rival type best-responds: $q_2(c) = (12 - q_1 - c)/2$. At $\mu = \tfrac12$, the expected rival output is $\mathbb{E}[q_2] = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{12 - q_1}{2} + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{6 - q_1}{2} = 4.5 - q_1/2$.

Firm 1's first-order condition, treating $\mathbb{E}[q_2]$ as its rival's output, is $q_1 = (12 - \mathbb{E}[q_2])/2$. Substituting, $q_1 = (12 - 4.5 + q_1/2)/2 = 3.75 + q_1/4$, so $q_1 = 5$. Then $q_2(\text{low}) = (12 - 5)/2 = 3.5$ and $q_2(\text{high}) = (12 - 5 - 6)/2 = 0.5$. Firm 1 never learns the cost; it just plays against the average.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Under incomplete information you best-respond to the distribution of the other player's types, not to any single one. Each of your own types plays its own best response, and all of them together form the Bayes-Nash equilibrium.</Callout>
</Section>
