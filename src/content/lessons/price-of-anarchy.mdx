# Price of anarchy and Braess's paradox

<p class="lead">Equilibrium does not mean efficient. The price of anarchy measures how much selfish behaviour costs compared with a coordinated optimum. For traffic with linear costs the waste is capped at a third, but the direction of "improvement" can be counterintuitive: removing options sometimes helps, and adding them can hurt.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each driver chases their own fastest route, ignoring the delay they add to others. The free shortcut looks individually attractive, so everyone floods onto it, overloading the congestible segments it connects. At the new equilibrium no one can improve alone, yet all are worse off than before the road existed. The gap between what selfish play produces and what coordination could achieve is the price of anarchy, and Braess shows adding options can widen it.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="price of anarchy">
The price of anarchy is the ratio of the total cost at the worst Nash equilibrium to the cost of the socially optimal outcome. It measures the inefficiency of selfish behaviour. For routing with affine (linear) cost functions, Roughgarden and Tardos proved the price of anarchy is at most $4/3$: selfish routing is never more than a third worse than optimal.
</DefinitionAside>

**Pigou.** One unit of traffic chooses between a fixed link (cost $1$) and a congestible link (cost equal to its load). Selfishly, everyone takes the congestible link (cost $1$, tied with the fixed one) for total cost $1$. The optimum splits traffic in half, giving total cost $\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(\tfrac12) = 0.75$. The price of anarchy is $1 / 0.75 = 4/3$.

**Braess.** Four thousand drivers, two routes each costing $\tfrac{x}{100} + 45$. Before any shortcut, traffic splits $2000/2000$, so each pays $\tfrac{2000}{100} + 45 = 65$. Add a zero-cost shortcut linking the two congestible halves: every driver now takes both congestible segments, and at equilibrium each pays $\tfrac{4000}{100} + \tfrac{4000}{100} = 80 > 65$. The extra road made the equilibrium worse.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Pigou's 4/3 and Braess's 65 to 80">
**Pigou.** With load $f$ on the congestible link, total cost is $(1-f)\cdot 1 + f\cdot f$. Selfish drivers push $f$ to $1$ (the congestible link never looks worse than the fixed one to an individual), giving cost $1$. Minimizing $(1-f) + f^2$ gives $f = \tfrac12$ and cost $0.75$. Ratio $4/3$.

**Braess.** Before: each route carries 2000, cost $2000/100 + 45 = 65$. After the shortcut, the dominant route for each driver uses two congestible segments each carrying all 4000, so each driver's time is $40 + 40 = 80$. Checking it is an equilibrium: deviating to an old pure route would cost $40 + 45 = 85 > 80$, so no one leaves. The free road raised everyone's time from 65 to 80.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Selfish routing is inefficient but boundedly so: with linear costs the price of anarchy is at most 4/3. Yet adding a road can make an equilibrium strictly worse for everyone, which is Braess's paradox.</Callout>
</Section>
