# Cheap talk

<p class="lead">Signaling worked because the signal was costly. Here messages are free: an expert can say anything. You might think free words carry no information, but they can, partially. How much gets through depends on how far the expert's interests diverge from yours.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The expert cannot lie freely and still be believed: you know the bias and discount accordingly. But if the expert only has to place the state in a broad bracket, it has no reason to misrepresent which bracket, because within a bracket its preferred action and yours are close enough. So credible communication is coarse: the expert reveals a region, not a point. The wider the bias, the wider the brackets must be to keep the expert honest, until only one bracket is left and the message means nothing.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

The state $\theta$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. You want to choose an action equal to $\theta$; the expert wants $\theta + b$. Messages are costless, so the only equilibria are **partition equilibria**: the expert reveals which interval of a partition contains $\theta$, and you take the interval's midpoint as your action.

<DefinitionAside label="cheap talk (Crawford-Sobel)">
With costless messages and a conflict of interest $b$, informative equilibria partition the state space into finitely many intervals; the expert reports the interval, and the receiver best-responds with its mean. The maximum number of intervals falls as $b$ rises, and for $b \ge 1/4$ (in the uniform-quadratic case) only the one-interval babbling equilibrium survives.
</DefinitionAside>

Consider the two-interval equilibrium with boundary type $a_1$. The intervals $[0,a_1]$ and $[a_1,1]$ induce actions $y_1 = a_1/2$ and $y_2 = (a_1+1)/2$. The boundary type must be indifferent between the two induced actions, given its own target $a_1 + b$:

$$
2(a_1 + b) = y_1 + y_2 = a_1 + \tfrac12 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a_1 = \tfrac12 - 2b.
$$

At $b = \tfrac18$ this gives $a_1 = \tfrac14$: the expert can credibly say only whether the state is below or above $\tfrac14$. A two-interval equilibrium needs $a_1 > 0$, so it exists only when $b < \tfrac14$.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="The most informative equilibrium at b = 1/8">
Try two intervals split at $a_1$. The low interval $[0, a_1]$ induces the action $a_1/2$; the high interval $[a_1, 1]$ induces $(a_1 + 1)/2$. For the split to hold, the type exactly at $a_1$ must not prefer to be taken for the other interval. Its ideal action is $a_1 + b$, so indifference requires it to sit halfway between the two induced actions: $2(a_1 + b) = a_1/2 + (a_1+1)/2$.

Solving, $a_1 = \tfrac12 - 2b$. At $b = \tfrac18$, $a_1 = \tfrac14$, and the receiver's two actions are $0.125$ and $0.625$. Push the bias to $\tfrac14$ and $a_1$ hits $0$: the low interval vanishes and only babbling remains. Costless words inform, but a larger bias buys coarser talk, not the same message shifted.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Costless messages can still transmit information, but only coarsely, and the amount shrinks as the speaker's bias grows. Past a threshold, a biased expert can convey nothing credible at all.</Callout>
</Section>
