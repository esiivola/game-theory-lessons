# Mechanism design and the revelation principle

<p class="lead">Mechanism design runs game theory backward: fix the outcome you want, then build the rules that deliver it. The revelation principle is the key simplification. Any equilibrium outcome of any mechanism can be reproduced by a direct mechanism in which telling the truth is a best response, so you never have to search beyond honest, direct rules.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
In the first-price auction you shade because your bid sets your price. The designer can absorb that shading into the rules: instead of asking for a bid and making you shave it, ask for your value and charge you the payment your shaded bid would have produced. Facing that direct rule, you have no reason to lie, because any misreport just moves you to the outcome of a different type, which you did not prefer. The strategic shading is folded into the mechanism, leaving honesty optimal.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

With two bidders and values uniform on $[0,1]$, the first-price equilibrium bid is $b(v) = v/2$. A bidder of value $v$ wins when the rival's value is below $v$, which happens with probability $v$, and pays its bid $v/2$ when it wins. Its interim expected payment is

$$
m(v) = v \cdot \tfrac{v}{2} = \tfrac{v^2}{2}.
$$

The direct mechanism: each bidder reports a value, the highest report wins, and a bidder reporting $v$ pays $m(v) = v^2/2$ in expectation. This reproduces the same allocation and the same payments, and truth-telling is a best response.

<DefinitionAside label="the revelation principle">
For any mechanism and any Bayes-Nash equilibrium of it, there is a direct mechanism (players report their types) in which truthful reporting is an equilibrium and which yields the same outcome. So without loss of generality the designer can restrict attention to incentive-compatible, individually rational direct mechanisms. It characterises what is achievable; it says nothing about robustness or simplicity in practice.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="From shaded bids to truthful reports">
In the two-bidder first-price auction, a type $v$ bids $v/2$. Probability of winning is $\Pr(\text{rival} < v) = v$, and the payment on winning is the bid $v/2$, so the interim expected payment is $v \cdot v/2 = v^2/2$, and interim surplus is $v \cdot v - v^2/2 = v^2/2$.

Now build the direct mechanism: ask each bidder for a number, award the item to the highest, and charge a reporter of $v$ the amount $v^2/2$ in expectation. A bidder considering reporting $r \ne v$ would receive the win probability and payment of type $r$, but since $v$'s own bundle was already optimal in the original auction, deviating cannot help. Truth-telling is optimal, and every number, the allocation, the payment, the revenue, matches the first-price auction.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">The revelation principle lets a designer study only direct, truthful mechanisms without losing any achievable outcome. It is a lens for what is possible, not a claim that direct mechanisms are the best to run in practice.</Callout>
</Section>
