# Pure-strategy Nash equilibrium

<p class="lead">A <span class="in-acc">Nash equilibrium</span> is a set of choices where no player can do better by changing their own move alone. Find it by looking for cells that are a best response for everyone at once.</p>

## Find the stable cells

Tap a cell you think is stable, then reveal each player's best responses. A cell glows when it is a best response for both, and that is a Nash equilibrium.

<DefinitionAside label="Nash equilibrium">
A profile $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium if, for every player $i$, $u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $s_i$. In words: given what everyone else does, no one has a profitable unilateral move.
</DefinitionAside>

This game has two pure equilibria, both-opera and both-football. Both players prefer coordinating to missing each other, but they disagree about which cell to land on.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Mark best responses, then read off the equilibria">
Work one player at a time. **You** compare down each column. If they pick opera, your opera pays $2$ against your football's $0$, so mark opera. If they pick football, your football pays $1$ against $0$, so mark football. **They** compare across each row. If you pick opera, their opera pays $1$ against $0$; if you pick football, their football pays $2$ against $0$.

Now look for cells carrying both marks. $(\text{opera}, \text{opera})$ has yours and theirs; so does $(\text{football}, \text{football})$. Those two are the pure Nash equilibria. The off-diagonal cells pay $(0,0)$ and carry no marks at all: from either one, both players want to move.

**Faded.** Raise the mismatch payoff for missing each other from $0$ to $3$ in every off-diagonal cell, so the couple would rather be apart. Redo the marks: against their opera you now prefer football ($3 > 2$), and against their football you prefer opera ($3 > 1$). Their marks flip the same way, and the two equilibria move to the mismatched cells. The procedure did not change; only where the marks land.

**Solo.** Try the matrix from the dominance lesson (advertise pays $3$ and $6$, staying quiet pays $1$ and $4$). Mark both players' best replies and you will find exactly one cell with both marks. The next lesson is about what makes that cell uncomfortable.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">A Nash equilibrium is mutual best response, not group happiness. A game can have two, one, or none, and the stable outcome need not be the one everyone would pick together.</Callout>
</Section>
