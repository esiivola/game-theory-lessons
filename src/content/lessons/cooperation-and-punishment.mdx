# Cooperation and punishment

<p class="lead">The commons lesson predicted zero contribution. Real groups start out generous, then unravel, exactly as free-riding erodes trust. The twist experiments reveal: let players pay to punish free-riders, and cooperation climbs back up, even though punishing is individually costly.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Most people are conditional cooperators: they give if others do. A few free-ride, and each round the cooperators see themselves exploited and lower their giving to match, so contributions spiral down. Punishment breaks the spiral. Even though paying to dock a free-rider costs you and helps everyone (a second-order public good), enough people do it out of a taste for fairness that free-riding stops paying. Contributions recover, sustained by a threat that standard self-interest says no one should carry out.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

The stage game is the usual linear public good: with $n = 4$, endowment $20$, and multiplier $1.6$, the marginal per-capita return is $0.4 < 1$, so contributing $\$1$ returns only $\$0.40$ to you. The dominant contribution is $0$, yet all contributing fully would give each $\$32$ versus $\$20$ from hoarding, since the total return on each dollar, $4 \times 0.4 = 1.6$, exceeds $1$.

<DefinitionAside label="conditional cooperation and punishment">
Experimentally, contributions start around 40 to 60 percent of the endowment and decay over repeated rounds without punishment, as conditional cooperators withdraw in response to free-riders. Adding a costly punishment stage (pay $1$ to reduce a free-rider's payoff by $3$) reverses the decay: contributions rise and stay high. Punishing is itself a public good (it benefits everyone but costs the punisher), so pure self-interest predicts no punishment; that people punish anyway is the behavioral finding.
</DefinitionAside>

The standard theory has two failures here: it predicts zero contribution from the start (real groups begin generous) and no punishment (real people punish). Both gaps are explained by social preferences: conditional cooperation and a willingness to sacrifice to enforce fairness.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why punishment sustains giving">
Contributing $\$1$ grows the pot by $\$1.60$, split four ways, so $\$0.40$ comes back to you: a private loss of $\$0.60$, and the money-maximizing choice is $\$0$. Without enforcement, conditional cooperators watch the free-rider keep everything and cut their own giving to avoid being suckers, and contributions decay toward zero.

Now let each player pay $\$1$ to cut a free-rider's payoff by $\$3$. A free-rider who kept $\$20$ but draws several punishments can end up below what a contributor earns, so free-riding no longer pays. Anticipating punishment, the free-rider contributes, and the cooperators, no longer exploited, keep giving. The punishment is costly and rarely needed on the equilibrium path, but its availability is what holds cooperation up.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Without enforcement, cooperation in a public good decays as free-riders erode trust. Costly punishment, which self-interest says no one should undertake, revives and sustains contributions in real groups.</Callout>
</Section>
