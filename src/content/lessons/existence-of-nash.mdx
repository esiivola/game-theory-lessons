# Existence of Nash equilibrium

<p class="lead">Matching Pennies had no equilibrium in fixed moves, but it had one in probabilities. Rock-Paper-Scissors makes you wonder whether the trick always works. It does. Nash proved that every finite game has at least one equilibrium, as long as you allow mixed strategies.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Any lopsided habit gives the bot a target. Play Rock too often and it plays Paper. The only frequency it cannot punish is exactly one-third each, with no pattern in the order, because then every counter it might try pays the same against you. That even mix is the mixed-strategy equilibrium of the game.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Rock-Paper-Scissors is symmetric and zero-sum, and it has no pure-strategy equilibrium. Try the symmetric mix: each player plays every move with probability $\tfrac13$.

Against that mix, any single move you make wins one-third of the time, loses one-third, and ties one-third, for an expected payoff of

$$
\tfrac13(1) + \tfrac13(-1) + \tfrac13(0) = 0.
$$

Every pure move earns $0$ against the uniform mix, so you are indifferent among all of them, and mixing $\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13$ is a best response to itself. That is the equilibrium.

<DefinitionAside label="Nash's existence theorem">
Every finite game (finitely many players, each with finitely many pure strategies) has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies. The proof looks for a fixed point of best response on the compact convex space of mixed profiles, because a profile that best-responds to itself is exactly an equilibrium. Best response is a correspondence, not a function (ties leave several best replies), so Nash's 1950 proof applies Kakutani's fixed-point theorem for correspondences; his 1951 proof instead builds a continuous map with the same fixed points and applies Brouwer's.
</DefinitionAside>

The theorem guarantees existence. It says nothing about how many equilibria there are, or which one gets played.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Verify the uniform mix is an equilibrium">
Fix the opponent at $\tfrac13$ each. Compute your payoff to Rock: it beats Scissors ($+1$, probability $\tfrac13$), loses to Paper ($-1$, probability $\tfrac13$), ties Rock ($0$, probability $\tfrac13$), so the expected payoff is $0$. The same holds for Paper and for Scissors, by symmetry.

Since all three pure moves pay $0$, any mix of them also pays $0$, so you cannot do better than the uniform mix. Neither can the opponent. Both playing $\tfrac13$ each is therefore a Nash equilibrium.

It is also the only one, and symmetry alone does not show that. Suppose you play Rock, Paper, Scissors with probabilities $r, p, s$. The opponent's payoff to Rock is $s - p$, to Paper is $r - s$, to Scissors is $p - r$. For your mix to be part of an equilibrium it must hold them to the game's value of $0$, so all three must be at most zero: $s \le p$, $r \le s$, $p \le r$. That chain closes on itself, forcing $r = p = s = \tfrac13$.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Allow mixed strategies and no finite game is ever unsolvable. There is always at least one equilibrium; the open question is which of them, if any, players actually reach.</Callout>
</Section>
