# Adverse selection and screening

<p class="lead">Hidden information about types, not hidden actions, is the problem here. When sellers know quality and buyers do not, any price attracts the worst goods that price will tempt out. Buyers know this and shade what they will pay, which drives even more of the good goods away, until the market can unravel completely.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The price does not just set the terms of trade; it selects which cars appear. Offer a high price and you attract cars up to that quality, but the ones you draw in are, on average, only half as good as the price suggests, because quality is spread out below it. Buyers anticipate this and will pay only three-quarters of the price for the average car on offer. That shortfall holds at every price, so trade never happens. The information gap, not any villainy, empties the market.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Quality $q$ is uniform on $[0,100]$. A seller lists a car only if the price $p$ covers its value, so the cars on the market are those with $q \le p$, whose average quality is $p/2$. Buyers value a car at $1.5q$, so their willingness to pay for the market's average car is

$$
1.5 \cdot \tfrac{p}{2} = 0.75\,p < p \quad\text{for all } p > 0.
$$

Willingness to pay is below the price at every positive price, so the only price at which the market clears is $p = 0$, where nothing of value trades. This is complete unraveling.

<DefinitionAside label="adverse selection and screening">
Adverse selection is a hidden-type problem: the informed side (the seller) self-selects in a way that worsens what the uninformed side gets. It is distinct from moral hazard, which is a hidden-action problem. One remedy is screening: the uninformed party offers a menu designed so that different types choose different options. In insurance (Rothschild-Stiglitz), a menu of contracts trading premium against deductible leads low-risk and high-risk buyers to self-select, separating the types without anyone announcing them.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why no price clears, and how screening escapes it">
Fix any price $p > 0$. Sellers with quality above $p$ keep their cars, so only qualities in $[0,p]$ are offered, averaging $p/2$. A buyer values that average car at $1.5 \times p/2 = 0.75p$. Since $0.75p < p$, the buyer refuses, so demand collapses at every positive price and the market clears only at $0$.

**Screening** turns the problem around. Instead of one price, the uninformed side offers a menu. In insurance, pair a low premium with a high deductible and a high premium with a low deductible. Low-risk buyers, who rarely claim, prefer the cheap high-deductible plan; high-risk buyers prefer the fuller coverage. Each type reveals itself by its choice, so the menu separates types that a single price could not.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When the informed side self-selects on hidden quality, raising the price mostly buys worse goods, so buyers pay less and the good goods leave. A well-designed menu can screen the types apart where a single price cannot.</Callout>
</Section>
