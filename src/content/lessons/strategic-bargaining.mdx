# Strategic bargaining

<p class="lead">The axiomatic answer said what a fair split looks like. Rubinstein asked how bargaining actually plays out when offers alternate and delay is costly. The result is sharp: a single subgame-perfect split, agreed immediately, set by who loses less from waiting.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Why settle at once instead of haggling for rounds? Because both sides can see how it ends. If you lowball, the responder rejects, becomes the proposer next round, and claims the proposer's share of a slightly smaller pie. Knowing that, you offer them exactly what waiting is worth to them, no more, and they accept immediately. Delay only shrinks the pie, so no one chooses it in equilibrium.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Let the proposer's equilibrium share be $x$. By symmetry of the stationary game, if the responder rejects, they become the proposer and claim $x$ of a pie discounted to $\delta$, worth $\delta x$ to them now. So the proposer must leave them at least $\delta x$:

$$
x = 1 - \delta x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{1}{1+\delta}.
$$

At $\delta = 0.9$ the proposer keeps $\tfrac{1}{1.9} \approx 0.526$: barely more than half. The advantage of moving first vanishes as players get patient.

<DefinitionAside label="Rubinstein bargaining">
With alternating offers and a common discount factor $\delta \in (0,1)$, the infinite-horizon game has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium: the first proposer offers $(\tfrac{1}{1+\delta}, \tfrac{\delta}{1+\delta})$ and it is accepted at once. With different discount factors the proposer's share is $\tfrac{1-\delta_2}{1-\delta_1\delta_2}$: the more patient side gets more.
</DefinitionAside>

As $\delta \to 1$ the split approaches $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, matching the Nash bargaining solution with equal footing. Two different questions, the axiomatic and the strategic, converge on the same answer. That link is the Nash program.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Solve for the split, then vary patience">
The proposer leaves the responder their continuation value. If the responder rejects, they propose next round and keep $x$ of a pie worth $\delta$, so their continuation is $\delta x$. The proposer keeps the rest: $x = 1 - \delta x$, giving $x = 1/(1+\delta)$.

At $\delta = 0.5$ the proposer keeps $1/1.5 \approx 0.667$; at $\delta = 0.9$, about $0.526$; at $\delta \to 1$, exactly $0.5$. Patience compresses the first-mover advantage.

With unequal patience, the proposer (player 1) keeps $\tfrac{1 - \delta_2}{1 - \delta_1 \delta_2}$. If player 1 is very patient ($\delta_1$ near 1) and player 2 is not, player 1 takes almost the whole pie. Relative patience is the source of bargaining power.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Alternating-offer bargaining ends in the first round at a split set by patience: the proposer keeps 1/(1+delta). The one who loses less from waiting wins the larger share.</Callout>
</Section>
