# Global games

<p class="lead">Coordination games have many equilibria, and stories about crises often lean on that: the attack succeeds because everyone expects it to. Global games puncture the mystery. Add a small amount of private noise to each player's information and the multiplicity collapses to a single equilibrium pinned by the fundamentals, not by mood.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
With everyone perfectly informed of the same fundamental, there is a middle range where both "all attack" and "none attack" are equilibria, and the outcome seems arbitrary. Private noise breaks the symmetry: no one is quite sure what others saw, so each must reason about the whole distribution of signals. That reasoning ties every player to a single switching point. A cheaper attack lowers the bar for finding it worthwhile, so the threshold at which the regime survives moves up, and more economies fall.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

The regime collapses if the mass of attackers reaches the fundamental $\theta$; attacking costs $c$ and pays off only if the regime falls. Under complete information, for intermediate $\theta$ there are two equilibria (everyone attacks, or no one does). Now let each speculator $i$ see a private signal $x_i = \theta + \text{noise}$.

<DefinitionAside label="global game">
A global game is a coordination game where each player observes the payoff-relevant state with a small idiosyncratic error. As the noise shrinks, the game has a unique equilibrium in threshold strategies: attack iff your signal is below a cutoff. The uniqueness comes from the noise, which prevents beliefs about others from being common knowledge and forces iterated reasoning to a single outcome.
</DefinitionAside>

At the switching signal, the marginal speculator's belief about the fraction of others attacking is uniform on $[0,1]$ (the Laplacian property), so it puts probability $1 - \theta^*$ on the regime falling. Setting that equal to the cost $c$ gives the fundamental threshold

$$
\theta^* = 1 - c.
$$

The regime falls exactly when $\theta < \theta^*$. With $c = \tfrac12$, $\theta^* = \tfrac12$. Lower the cost and $\theta^*$ rises, so crises become more common: strong fundamentals no longer guarantee safety once they dip below $1-c$.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Find the crisis threshold">
Consider the marginal speculator, the one whose signal sits exactly at the switching cutoff. A key property of the global-games limit is that this agent's posterior over the fraction $\ell$ of others who attack is uniform on $[0,1]$. Its best estimate of the fundamental is its own signal, $\theta^*$, and the regime falls when $\ell \ge \theta^*$, an event of probability $1 - \theta^*$.

Attacking pays $1$ if the regime falls and costs $c$ either way, so the marginal agent is indifferent when $1 - \theta^* = c$, giving the fundamental threshold $\theta^* = 1 - c$. At $c = \tfrac12$ this is $\tfrac12$: economies with $\theta < \tfrac12$ collapse, those above survive. Halve the cost to $c = \tfrac14$ and $\theta^* = \tfrac34$: now three-quarters of economies are vulnerable. Cheaper attacking makes crises more likely.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Small private noise selects a unique threshold equilibrium out of a coordination game's many equilibria. Crises are not pure sunspots: the regime falls exactly when fundamentals drop below 1 minus the cost of attacking.</Callout>
</Section>
