# Schelling and focal points

<p class="lead">A game can have several equilibria that pay exactly the same, and then the theory shrugs: it cannot say which one you will land on. Yet people coordinate anyway, and they do it well. The tie-breaker is not in the payoffs. It is in what stands out.</p>

<Section id="play">
## Predict, then play

Here is the game stripped bare. You and a stranger each call Heads or Tails, with no way to talk. You both score only if you match. The two of you have no reason to prefer one over the other, so what do you do?

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
You did not reason about payoffs, because the payoffs were a tie. You reasoned about the other person: what will look obvious to them? Heads comes before Tails, so it feels like the natural call, and because it feels that way to almost everyone, choosing it is the smart move. A shared sense of what stands out does the coordinating that the payoffs cannot.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

A pure coordination game is a game where the players' interests line up perfectly, but there is more than one way to line up. In the 2x2 case both matching cells are Nash equilibria and both pay the same:

$$
u_1(H,H) = u_1(T,T) > u_1(H,T) = u_1(T,H),
$$

and the same for player 2. Nash equilibrium narrows the answer to the two matching cells. It cannot go further, because the two are identical in every payoff. Any theory that looks only at payoffs is stuck at a tie.

<DefinitionAside label="focal point">
A focal point (or Schelling point) is an equilibrium that players tend to select because something outside the payoffs, a label, a tradition, a roundness, a shared frame, makes it stand out as the obvious choice. It coordinates expectations without changing a single payoff.
</DefinitionAside>

The lesson generalizes. Meeting in New York, Schelling's respondents converged on Grand Central at noon. Asked to split \$100 by matching, people say 50/50. Asked to name a positive number, many say 1. In each case salience, not optimization, picks the equilibrium.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why theory cannot break the tie">
Write the coordination game: matching pays $(1,1)$, mismatching pays $(0,0)$. Mark best responses. If the other player picks Heads, your best reply is Heads; if they pick Tails, yours is Tails. So $(H,H)$ and $(T,T)$ are both Nash equilibria.

Now try to rank them. Every payoff in $(H,H)$ equals the matching payoff in $(T,T)$, so no efficiency or fairness argument distinguishes them. The selection has to come from outside the game: which label feels obvious. That is the focal point, and it is a rational response to needing to match, not a lapse in reasoning.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When equilibria are tied on payoffs, players coordinate on whatever stands out. The focal point lives outside the math, but choosing it is the rational thing to do.</Callout>
</Section>
