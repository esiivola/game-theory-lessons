# Information design and Bayesian persuasion

<p class="lead">Cheap talk failed because the speaker could say anything after learning the truth. Flip that: let the sender commit to an information policy in advance, before the state is known, and honesty becomes powerful. By choosing what evidence to generate, a sender can shift a rational receiver's beliefs in its favour without ever misreporting.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The trick is committing before the fact. The prosecutor is not lying about any case: a "guilty" report genuinely raises the odds of guilt. But by mixing in just enough innocents, the prosecutor makes "guilty" reports common while keeping each one exactly convincing enough to convict. Report too many innocents and the judge stops believing the report; report too few and you waste convincing power you did not need. The optimum sits right at the judge's threshold.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

The prior is $\Pr(\text{guilty}) = 0.3$, and the judge convicts iff the posterior is at least $\tfrac12$. The signal reports "guilty" always when guilty, and with probability $x$ when innocent. By Bayes, a "guilty" report yields posterior

$$
\Pr(\text{guilty}\mid\text{"guilty"}) = \frac{0.3}{0.3 + 0.7\,x}.
$$

Set this to the threshold $\tfrac12$: $0.3 = 0.5(0.3 + 0.7x)$ gives $x = \tfrac{3}{7}$. Then the chance of a "guilty" report is $0.3 + 0.7\cdot\tfrac37 = 0.6$, and every one convicts. Conviction rate $60\%$ from a $30\%$ guilt rate, with no dishonest report.

<DefinitionAside label="Bayesian persuasion">
The sender commits to a signal (a mapping from states to message probabilities) before the state is realized, and the receiver best-responds to the posterior each message induces. Any signal is feasible as long as the message-posteriors average back to the prior (Bayes plausibility). The sender's problem is to pick the best such distribution of posteriors, which is read off the concave closure of the sender's payoff. Commitment, absent in cheap talk, is what makes honest persuasion work.
</DefinitionAside>

The two extremes lose. Full disclosure convicts only the truly guilty, $30\%$. Secrecy leaves the posterior at the prior $0.3 < 0.5$, convicting no one. Honest partial disclosure beats both.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Push the posterior to the threshold">
Let the signal flag an innocent as "guilty" with probability $x$. A "guilty" report then has posterior $0.3 / (0.3 + 0.7x)$. For the judge to convict, this must reach $\tfrac12$, and to convict as often as possible the prosecutor wants $x$ as large as it can be while still hitting $\tfrac12$ exactly.

Solve $0.3/(0.3 + 0.7x) = 0.5$: multiply out to $0.3 = 0.15 + 0.35x$, so $x = 3/7 \approx 0.43$. The probability of a "guilty" report is $0.3 + 0.7 \cdot \tfrac37 = 0.6$. Every such report convicts, so the conviction rate is $60\%$. Raising $x$ past $3/7$ drops the posterior below $\tfrac12$ and the judge stops convicting; lowering it wastes convincing power. The optimum sits exactly at the threshold.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Commit to an honest information policy in advance and you can move a rational receiver far beyond the prior. The optimal signal is just convincing enough, pushing beliefs exactly to the receiver's threshold and no further.</Callout>
</Section>
