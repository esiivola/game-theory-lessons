# The nucleolus

<p class="lead">When the core is empty, no allocation is unblockable, so stability alone cannot choose. The nucleolus picks the allocation that minimizes the largest complaint, then, among those, the next-largest, and so on. It always exists, is unique, and reconstructs a famous ancient bankruptcy rule.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
With an empty core, someone always has a grievance: every pair could have grabbed the whole prize, so any pair that is underpaid feels cheated. The nucleolus does not try to eliminate complaints (impossible here) but to be as fair as possible about them, shrinking the loudest one first. In a symmetric game that means treating everyone alike, so the equal split leaves the biggest complaint smallest. Push any share up and some pair's complaint grows.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="the nucleolus">
For an allocation $x$, each coalition $S$ has an excess $e(S, x) = v(S) - \sum_{i \in S} x_i$, its complaint. Sort all excesses from largest to smallest. The nucleolus is the allocation that lexicographically minimizes this sorted vector: first minimize the largest excess, then among ties the next, and so on. It always exists, is unique, and lies in the core whenever the core is nonempty.
</DefinitionAside>

In the majority game, $v(S) = 1$ for any coalition of two or more. For a pair $\{i,j\}$ the excess is $1 - (x_i + x_j) = x_k$, the share of the excluded player. To minimize the largest such excess is to minimize $\max_k x_k$, which by symmetry and efficiency ($x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1$) is achieved at $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$.

The Talmud connection: for claims $(100, 200, 300)$ against an estate of $200$, the Talmud prescribes $(50, 75, 75)$, which is exactly the nucleolus of the associated bankruptcy game. Aumann and Maschler showed the ancient rule and the nucleolus agree across all the Talmud's cases.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Minimize the worst complaint">
In the majority game a pair's complaint equals the excluded player's share: the pair could have taken the whole $1$ but got $1 - x_k$, so its excess is $x_k$. The three pairwise complaints are therefore $x_1$, $x_2$, and $x_3$. The largest is $\max(x_1, x_2, x_3)$.

Minimizing that maximum, subject to $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1$, forces the shares equal: $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$, where the largest complaint is $\tfrac13$. Any deviation raises some share above $\tfrac13$, increasing the worst complaint. So the nucleolus is the equal split, which here matches the Shapley value because the game is symmetric. In asymmetric games, such as the Talmud estate, the nucleolus and Shapley value diverge.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">The nucleolus minimizes the largest complaint, then the next, giving a unique division even when the core is empty. It reconstructs the Talmud's bankruptcy rule, and coincides with the Shapley value only in symmetric games.</Callout>
</Section>
