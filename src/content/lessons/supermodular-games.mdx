# Supermodular games

<p class="lead">In some games your incentive to do more grows the more everyone else does. These strategic complements have a striking feature: many coordinated outcomes are all equilibria, ranked from best to worst, and fear of the low one can trap everyone there even though all would prefer the high one.</p>

## Predict, then play

You and three teammates each pick an effort from 1 to 7. Your payoff is $0.6 + 0.2\times(\text{group minimum}) - 0.1\times(\text{your own effort})$. The reward tracks the weakest link, so your effort helps only if it does not exceed everyone else's.

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Every unit of effort costs you, but the reward depends only on the smallest effort in the group. So raising yours above the minimum spends the cost and buys nothing. That makes your best move: match the lowest effort you expect. When you fear a teammate will play low, you play low too, and so does everyone, which is exactly how a group of people who would all prefer to work hard end up stuck working little.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

With payoff $u_i = 0.6 + 0.2\,\min_j e_j - 0.1\,e_i$, consider everyone choosing the same effort $x$. Then $\min_j e_j = x$ and each earns $0.6 + 0.1x$, rising in $x$. Every common effort from 1 to 7 is a Nash equilibrium, and they are Pareto-ranked: all-7 pays $1.3$, all-1 pays $0.7$.

Why are they all equilibria? Given the others at $x$, raising your effort leaves the minimum at $x$ (so no gain) but costs more; lowering it drops the minimum and your reward. So matching is a best response.

<DefinitionAside label="supermodular game (strategic complements)">
A game has strategic complements when each player's best response is nondecreasing in the others' actions: the more they do, the more you want to do. Such games always have a largest and a smallest equilibrium, and every equilibrium is bracketed between them. Coordination is self-reinforcing in both directions, which is why both the good and the bad outcome are stable.
</DefinitionAside>

The danger is selection. All-7 is best for everyone, but it is risky: one cautious teammate and your high effort is wasted. All-1 is safe. Strategic uncertainty, not payoffs, is what pulls groups toward the low equilibrium.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why matching is the best response">
Suppose you expect the others' minimum to be $m$. If you play $e > m$, the group minimum is still $m$, so your payoff is $0.6 + 0.2m - 0.1e$, which falls as $e$ rises. If you play $e < m$, the minimum drops to $e$, giving $0.6 + 0.2e - 0.1e = 0.6 + 0.1e$, which is lower than matching at $m$. So the best response is $e = m$: match the minimum you expect.

Because that best response increases with what others do, the equilibria line up from all-1 to all-7. All-7 gives everyone $1.3$; all-1 gives $0.7$. Everyone prefers all-7, yet all-1 is perfectly stable, held in place by the fear that someone will slack.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When efforts are complements, best responses move together, so coordinated outcomes stack up as equilibria. The best one is fragile: fear of a weak link can lock everyone into the worst.</Callout>
</Section>
