# Auctions in the wild

<p class="lead">Auction theory earns its keep in two of the biggest markets built by economists: government spectrum sales and online advertising. Both show the theory in tension with practice, especially the generalized second price, which every major search engine uses despite it not being truthful.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
The generalized second price looks like the truthful second-price auction, and for a single slot it is. But with several slots, the price you pay depends on the bids around you, and a bidder can sometimes do better by shading or even preferring a cheaper lower slot. So truthful bidding is not an equilibrium. What saves it is that its stable, envy-free equilibrium lands on the efficient assignment and the VCG prices, so the outcome is sound even though the rule is not strategy-proof. Spectrum auctions face harder problems still: goods that are complements, where bidding on pieces risks the exposure problem.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real episodes

<DefinitionAside label="spectrum and ad auctions">
The FCC has auctioned spectrum since 1994 using simultaneous multiple-round designs; the 2016-17 incentive auction repurposed broadcast spectrum, paying broadcasters to give it up and reselling it: $19.8 billion raised, $10.05 billion paid to broadcasters, and more than $7 billion to the Treasury. Online advertising uses the generalized second price: advertisers are ranked by bid and each pays the next bid down per click. It is not truthful with multiple slots, but its lowest envy-free equilibrium reproduces the efficient VCG outcome.
</DefinitionAside>

Two practical wrinkles. Ad auctions run billions of times a day, so even small design choices (reserves, quality scores) move enormous revenue, and the GSP's non-truthfulness means bidders and platforms both invest in bid optimization. Spectrum auctions add combinatorial structure: licenses are worth more together, so bidding on a subset exposes a firm to winning a useless fragment (the exposure problem), which package-bidding designs (Ausubel-Milgrom) address.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Why GSP is not truthful, yet works">
Take two slots and advertisers valuing clicks at 10, 6, 4. VCG charges each the externality it imposes: the top advertiser pays 500, the second 200. Under GSP with truthful bids, the top slot's price is the next bid (6) over its 100 clicks, so 600, more than VCG's 500. So a truthful top bidder overpays, and would rather shade.

Advertisers therefore adjust until no one envies another's slot-and-price. That envy-free equilibrium assigns slots efficiently and charges exactly the VCG amounts. So the search engine gets the efficient, well-priced outcome VCG would give, through a simpler-looking rule, even though bidding your value is not itself optimal. That gap is why advertisers optimize bids and why the theory matters in practice.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Spectrum and ad auctions are auction theory at industrial scale. The generalized second price is not truthful with multiple slots, but its envy-free equilibrium reproduces the efficient VCG outcome, which is why it endures.</Callout>
</Section>
