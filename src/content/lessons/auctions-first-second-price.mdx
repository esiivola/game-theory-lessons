# Auctions: first and second price

<p class="lead">You value an item, your rivals value it too, and nobody sees anyone else's number. How you should bid depends on the rules. In a second-price auction, honesty is a dominant strategy. In a first-price auction, you must shave your bid. Yet on average the seller earns the same either way.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
In a second-price auction your bid decides only whether you win, not what you pay, so raising it above your value risks winning at a loss and lowering it only forfeits good wins. Bidding your true value is exactly right, whatever others do. In a first-price auction your bid also sets your price, so you shade below your value, trading a lower chance of winning for a better deal when you do. The two adjustments cancel: what you save by shading in the first-price auction, you give back in a higher chance of paying, and the seller nets the same.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="second-price (Vickrey) auction">
The highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid. Bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy: your bid affects only whether you win, never the price, so you want to win exactly when your value exceeds the price and to lose otherwise, which truthful bidding achieves.
</DefinitionAside>

With three bidders valuing 40, 70, and 90, the 90-bidder wins and pays 70, keeping a surplus of 20. No one gains by misreporting.

For the **first-price** auction with $n$ bidders and values drawn uniformly, the symmetric equilibrium shades each bid to

$$
b(v) = \frac{n-1}{n}\,v.
$$

With $n = 3$, you bid $\tfrac23 v$. You pay less when you win, but you win less often. Computing expected seller revenue for values uniform on $[0,1]$ gives $\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}$ in both formats: for $n = 3$, that is $\tfrac12$.

<DefinitionAside label="revenue equivalence">
Under independent private values, any auction that (in equilibrium) always gives the item to the highest-value bidder and gives a bidder with the lowest possible value zero expected surplus raises the same expected revenue. First-price and second-price auctions both qualify, so they tie.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Bid truthfully, then shade">
**Second-price.** Your value is 60. If you bid 60 and the top rival bid is 45, you win and pay 45, surplus 15. Bidding 80 instead changes nothing when you would have won anyway, and risks winning at a price above 60 (a loss). Bidding 50 only makes you lose auctions you would have profited from. So 60, your value, is optimal no matter what others do.

**First-price.** Now your bid is your price, so bidding 60 on a value of 60 leaves you zero surplus even when you win. With two rivals the equilibrium shades to $b = \tfrac23 \times 60 = 40$. You win less often but earn a margin when you do. Averaged out, the seller collects the same $\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}$ of the value scale as in the second-price auction.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Second-price auctions make honesty dominant; first-price auctions make you shade to (n-1)/n of your value. Despite the different behavior, the seller's expected revenue is identical.</Callout>
</Section>
