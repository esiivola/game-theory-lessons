# Nash bargaining

<p class="lead">Two parties can split a surplus many ways. Nash asked a different question: not how bargaining unfolds, but what a fair, sensible split should satisfy. A few reasonable axioms pin down a single answer, and it turns out to hinge on what each side gets if the talks collapse.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Your outside option is what you can walk away with, so it sets a floor on any deal you would accept. Improve that floor and you can credibly refuse worse offers, which pulls the settlement in your direction. It feels backwards at first, a stronger fallback sounds like less need to bargain, but in fact it is leverage: the other side must give you more to keep you at the table.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Let the pie be 1, your share $x$, theirs $1 - x$, and the disagreement point $d = (d_1, d_2)$, what each gets if talks fail. The Nash bargaining solution maximizes the Nash product

$$
(x - d_1)\,(1 - x - d_2).
$$

Differentiating and setting to zero gives

$$
x^* = \frac{1 + d_1 - d_2}{2}.
$$

With $d = (0,0)$ this is $\tfrac12$, an even split. With $d_1 = 0.2$ and $d_2 = 0$ it is $0.6$: your stronger fallback earns you more.

<DefinitionAside label="the four axioms">
Nash showed this is the unique split satisfying: efficiency (no waste, the whole pie is divided), symmetry (if the two sides are interchangeable, they split evenly), invariance to affine rescaling of either party's utility, and independence of irrelevant alternatives (removing unchosen options does not change the outcome). One split meets all four: the one that maximizes the Nash product.
</DefinitionAside>

This is an axiomatic answer: it says what a fair split looks like, not how haggling reaches it. The next lesson gives the strategic story.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Maximize the Nash product">
With $d = (0,0)$, maximize $x(1 - x)$. The derivative $1 - 2x = 0$ gives $x = \tfrac12$: the even split.

Now let your outside option be $d_1 = 0.2$ (theirs still 0). Maximize $(x - 0.2)(1 - x)$. The derivative is $(1 - x) - (x - 0.2) = 1.2 - 2x$, zero at $x = 0.6$. Your improved fallback raised your share from $0.5$ to $0.6$. The other side's collapse value, not their need, is what shrank their piece.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">The Nash solution splits the surplus to maximize the product of each side's gain over its walk-away value. A better outside option is bargaining power: it wins you a larger share, not a smaller one.</Callout>
</Section>
