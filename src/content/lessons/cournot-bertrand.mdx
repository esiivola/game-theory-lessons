# Cournot and Bertrand

<p class="lead">Give two firms the same product and one market, and how they compete depends on what they choose. Set quantities, as in Cournot, and they land between monopoly and perfect competition. Set prices, as in Bertrand, and a single undercut drives profit to zero.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each firm treats the other's output as fixed and asks: given that, how much should I make? The answer is always a bit more than the cooperative share, because expanding steals revenue from the rival while only slightly lowering the price you get. Both reason this way, so both expand, and the extra output drags the price down for everyone. Under price competition the same logic is sharper: undercutting the rival by a penny wins the entire market, so both keep cutting until price equals cost.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Firm 1 chooses $q_1$ to maximize profit $(120 - q_1 - q_2)\,q_1$, taking $q_2$ as given. The first-order condition is

$$
120 - 2q_1 - q_2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad q_1 = \frac{120 - q_2}{2}.
$$

That is firm 1's reaction curve. By symmetry firm 2's is $q_2 = (120 - q_1)/2$. Solving both, or imposing $q_1 = q_2$, gives

$$
q_1 = q_2 = 40, \quad Q = 80, \quad P = 40, \quad \pi_1 = \pi_2 = 1600.
$$

A monopolist would set $Q = 60$, $P = 60$, and earn $3600$. The duopoly produces more and earns less in total, and as you add firms the price keeps falling toward marginal cost.

<DefinitionAside label="the Bertrand paradox">
If the same two firms compete on price with identical products and equal marginal cost $c$, the unique Nash equilibrium is $p_1 = p_2 = c$, with zero profit. Any price above cost invites the rival to undercut and take the whole market, so competition collapses margins entirely, even with just two firms. Differentiated products or capacity limits restore positive margins; the paradox is the homogeneous-goods benchmark.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Solve Cournot, then change the cost">
Firm 1 maximizes $(120 - q_1 - q_2) q_1$. Differentiate in $q_1$ and set to zero: $120 - 2q_1 - q_2 = 0$. Impose symmetry $q_1 = q_2 = q$: $120 - 3q = 0$, so $q = 40$ and total output is $80$, price $40$, profit $40 \times 40 = 1600$ each.

**Faded.** Now let marginal cost be $c = 30$. The condition becomes $120 - 2q_1 - q_2 - 30 = 0$, and with symmetry $90 - 3q = 0$, so $q = 30$ each. Higher cost, lower output.

**Solo.** In the next lesson one firm gets to commit first. Guess: does moving first and committing to a large quantity help or hurt the leader?
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Competition on quantity lands between monopoly and cost; competition on price, with identical goods, drives all the way to cost. What firms choose to compete over shapes the outcome as much as how many of them there are.</Callout>
</Section>
