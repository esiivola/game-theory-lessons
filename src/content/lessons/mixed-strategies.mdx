# Mixed strategies and mixed Nash

<p class="lead">Some games have no resting point in fixed moves. Matching Pennies is the clean case: you want to match, they want to differ, so whatever you settle on, one of you wants to jump. The way out is to stop choosing a move and start choosing the odds.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
You do not mix to protect yourself directly. You mix to leave the opponent with no reason to prefer one move over the other. Once Heads and Tails pay them exactly the same, they cannot exploit you, whatever they do. That is the whole trick: choose the odds that make the other side indifferent.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

A **mixed strategy** for player $i$ is a probability distribution over their pure strategies. In Matching Pennies, yours is a single number: the probability $p$ you play Heads.

<DefinitionAside label="mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium">
A profile of mixed strategies is a Nash equilibrium if each player's mix is a best response to the others'. A player who mixes between several moves must be **indifferent** among them: each move in the mix earns the same expected payoff, or they would drop the worse one.
</DefinitionAside>

Suppose the opponent plays Heads with probability $q$. Your expected payoff to Heads is $q(1) + (1-q)(-1) = 2q - 1$, and to Tails it is $q(-1) + (1-q)(1) = 1 - 2q$. For you to be willing to mix, these must be equal:

$$
2q - 1 = 1 - 2q \quad\Rightarrow\quad q = \tfrac{1}{2}.
$$

By symmetry your own mix is $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Note what pinned it down: your opponent's indifference sets *your* probability, not your own payoff. You are indifferent at the equilibrium; the mix is there to discipline them.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Matching Pennies, then a game where 50/50 is wrong">
**Matching Pennies.** Set the opponent's Heads probability to $q$. Your Heads pays $2q-1$, your Tails pays $1-2q$. Equal at $q=\tfrac12$, so each of you plays $\tfrac12$, and the value of the game is $0$.

**Battle of the Sexes.** Opera/Opera pays $(2,1)$, Football/Football pays $(1,2)$, mismatches pay $(0,0)$. To make the column player indifferent, set your Opera probability $p$ so that their Opera payoff equals their Football payoff: $p\cdot 1 = (1-p)\cdot 2$, giving $p = \tfrac{2}{3}$. So the mixing answer is not always 50/50. Here you lean toward your preferred outcome two-thirds of the time.

**Solo.** In Chicken, two drivers head at each other. If both swerve they each get $6$; if both go straight they crash for $0$; if one holds firm while the other swerves, the bold driver gets $7$ and the timid one $2$. Pick your probability of going Straight to make the other driver indifferent between Swerve and Straight. Setting $6 - 4q = 7 - 7q$ gives Straight with probability $\tfrac{1}{3}$.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="reality">
## A note from the real world

<Callout kind="reality-check">
Equilibrium says mix to a precise fraction. Real people do not. In first plays of new games they lean on hunches and simple reasoning, not the exact indifference point, which is why crowds are predictable in ways theory says they should not be. The Behavioral unit picks this up with models of limited reasoning. Professionals under pressure, though, get close: penalty takers and keepers mix at rates strikingly near the theory (Palacios-Huerta, 2003).
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">You mix to make the other player indifferent, not to hedge yourself. At the equilibrium mix you are indifferent, and that is exactly why you cannot be exploited.</Callout>
</Section>
