# Implementation and Myerson-Satterthwaite

<p class="lead">Mechanism design asks what outcomes rules can implement. Sometimes the answer is a hard no. When a buyer and a seller each hold private information, Myerson and Satterthwaite proved that no mechanism can always trade when it should, break even, and respect both parties' incentives. Some efficient trades are simply unreachable.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Each side has an incentive to misrepresent: the buyer wants to look less eager, the seller more reluctant, so each shades. That shading is individually rational, but collectively it means small gains from trade get squeezed out, because neither will move far enough to close a thin margin. There is no clever payment scheme that undoes this while keeping both parties honest and the budget balanced. The loss is baked into two-sided private information.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

Buyer value $v_b$ and seller cost $v_s$ are each uniform on $[0,1]$. The efficient rule trades whenever $v_b \ge v_s$, with first-best expected gains $\tfrac16$. In the Chatterjee-Samuelson linear equilibrium the buyer bids $\tfrac23 v_b + \tfrac1{12}$ and the seller asks $\tfrac23 v_s + \tfrac14$; trade happens when the bid meets the ask, which reduces to

$$
v_b \ge v_s + \tfrac14.
$$

So every efficient trade with $0 \le v_b - v_s < \tfrac14$ is missed. The linear mechanism realizes surplus $\tfrac{9}{64} \approx 0.141$, below the first-best $\tfrac16 \approx 0.167$.

<DefinitionAside label="Myerson-Satterthwaite and Maskin monotonicity">
Myerson-Satterthwaite: for bilateral trade with independent private values that overlap, no mechanism is simultaneously efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational, and budget balanced. Something must give. On the positive side, Maskin's theorem characterizes which social goals can be Nash-implemented: a rule is implementable only if it is Maskin monotonic (roughly, if an outcome is chosen and it rises in everyone's ranking, it is still chosen), plus a no-veto condition.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="The 1/4 gap and the lost surplus">
The buyer's bid $\tfrac23 v_b + \tfrac1{12}$ meets the seller's ask $\tfrac23 v_s + \tfrac14$ exactly when $\tfrac23(v_b - v_s) \ge \tfrac14 - \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac16$, that is $v_b - v_s \ge \tfrac14$. Trade requires a gap of at least a quarter.

Now the surplus. First-best trades whenever $v_b \ge v_s$, giving expected gains $\iint_{v_b \ge v_s} (v_b - v_s)\,dv_b\,dv_s = \tfrac16$. The linear equilibrium only trades above the $\tfrac14$ gap, capturing $\tfrac{9}{64} \approx 0.141$. The difference is the deadweight loss forced by two-sided private information, and no mechanism removes it entirely.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When both sides hold private information, efficiency, honesty, participation, and budget balance cannot all hold. Rational bargainers shade, thin gains from trade vanish, and some deals that should happen never do.</Callout>
</Section>
