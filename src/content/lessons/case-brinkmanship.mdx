# Deterrence and brinkmanship

<p class="lead">The Cuban Missile Crisis is often modeled as Chicken: two sides on a collision course, each hoping the other swerves, disaster if neither does. The model is contested, but it captures the core puzzle of deterrence, how to make a threat credible when executing it would be catastrophic for you too.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Both sides prefer to be the one who holds firm while the other swerves, so the pure equilibria are lopsided, and neither wants to be the swerver. A flat threat to hold no matter what is not credible, because if the other truly will not swerve, holding means catastrophe. Schelling's insight was that you do not need a credible threat of certain disaster; you need a credible risk of it. By committing to a course that raises the chance of an accidental slide into war, you make backing down the other side's best move, even though nobody chose the disaster outright.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The model, and its limits

Write the crisis as Chicken: mutual backing-down $(0,0)$, one holds and one swerves $(\pm 1)$, mutual holding a catastrophe $(-10,-10)$. There are two pure equilibria (one side holds, the other swerves) and a symmetric mixed one. In the mixed equilibrium each side holds firm with probability $p$ that makes the other indifferent between holding ($-10p + 1(1-p)$) and swerving ($-p$, since a swerver facing a holder loses $1$): $-10p + 1(1-p) = -p$, giving $p = 1/10 = 10\%$. Brinkmanship is committing to a positive probability of catastrophe large enough that the rival prefers to yield.

<DefinitionAside label="the threat that leaves something to chance">
Schelling argued that a threat of certain mutual destruction is not credible (you would not push the button), but a threat that creates a genuine risk of accidental escalation can be. By taking steps that partly remove your own control, a blockade, an alert posture, you commit to a probability of disaster, making the opponent's best response to concede. Caveat: modeling the crisis as static Chicken is contested; Dixit and others argue a dynamic, sequential-bargaining account fits the actual back-and-forth better. Treat Chicken as the entry point, not the last word.
</DefinitionAside>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Brinkmanship as a committed risk">
In the mixed equilibrium of this Chicken game, a side holds firm with probability $p$ chosen to make the opponent indifferent between holding and swerving. Setting the opponent's payoff to holding equal to swerving, $-10p + 1(1-p) = 0$, gives $p = 1/11$, about a $9\%$ chance of catastrophe on each side. That small but real risk is what deters.

Brinkmanship operationalizes this: rather than threaten certain war (incredible), a leader takes actions that raise the chance of an uncontrolled slide, a naval blockade, forces on hair-trigger alert, so that the rival, facing a genuine probability of disaster, prefers to yield. Kennedy's blockade did exactly this, creating pressure without a first strike. The credibility came from the risk, not from a promise to end the world on purpose.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Deterrence in a game of nerve works not by threatening certain catastrophe, which is not credible, but by committing to a genuine risk of it. That is brinkmanship, and Chicken is a useful, if contested, model of the Cuban Missile Crisis.</Callout>
</Section>
