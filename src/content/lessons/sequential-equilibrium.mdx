# Sequential equilibrium and refinements

<p class="lead">Nash equilibrium, and even subgame perfection, can bless outcomes propped up by weakly dominated play that is never tested on the equilibrium path. Refinements ask more: an equilibrium should survive if players tremble, making each action with tiny probability by mistake. That extra demand throws out the equilibria that were only ever bluff.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
In $(B,R)$ both players choose an action that only pays off in cells that never occur, so nothing punishes them for it, and it counts as a Nash equilibrium. But the moment you admit the other side might tremble to Left, your Top action starts to earn something ($1$ in the rare Left column) while Bottom still earns nothing. So you strictly prefer Top, and $(B,R)$ unravels. The tremble tests actions that the equilibrium path would otherwise leave untested.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

In the game, $(B,R)$ is a Nash equilibrium only weakly: given the other plays Right, Bottom and Top both pay $0$, so Bottom is a best response but not a strict one, and it is weakly dominated by Top (which pays $1$ against Left and $0$ against Right). Introduce a tremble: the column player intends Right but plays Left with probability $\varepsilon$. Then Row earns $\varepsilon\cdot 1 = \varepsilon$ from Top and $0$ from Bottom, so for any $\varepsilon > 0$ Row strictly prefers Top, and $(B,R)$ is not robust.

<DefinitionAside label="trembling-hand and sequential equilibrium">
A trembling-hand perfect equilibrium is a limit, as the trembles vanish, of equilibria of perturbed games in which every action is played with strictly positive probability. Sequential equilibrium requires a strategy profile and beliefs that are the limit of totally-mixed (fully trembling) behavior, so beliefs are consistent even off the path. The refinements nest: every Nash equilibrium need not be subgame perfect, every subgame-perfect one need not be sequential, and

$$
\text{Nash} \supseteq \text{subgame-perfect} \supseteq \text{sequential} \supseteq \text{trembling-hand} \supseteq \text{proper}.
$$
</DefinitionAside>

Sequential equilibrium and PBE usually agree, but not always: sequential equilibrium's consistency requirement on off-path beliefs is stronger, ruling out belief systems PBE would permit (Selten's Horse is the classic separating example).
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Break the weak equilibrium with a tremble">
Take $(B,R)$. Given Right, Row is indifferent between Top and Bottom (both pay $0$), so Bottom is only a weak best response. Now let the column player tremble to Left with probability $\varepsilon > 0$. Row's payoff to Top becomes $\varepsilon\cdot 1 + (1-\varepsilon)\cdot 0 = \varepsilon$; to Bottom it stays $0$. Since $\varepsilon > 0$, Top is strictly better, so Row abandons Bottom and $(B,R)$ fails trembling-hand perfection.

Now $(T,L)$. Given Left trembling to Right with probability $\varepsilon$, Top pays $1-\varepsilon$ and Bottom pays $0$, so Top is strictly best; symmetrically Left is strictly best for the column player. $(T,L)$ survives every small tremble, so it is the trembling-hand perfect (and only sensible) equilibrium.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">Refinements keep only the equilibria that survive small mistakes. An equilibrium resting on a weakly dominated action, tested only by a tremble that "never happens", is exactly the kind these rules discard.</Callout>
</Section>
