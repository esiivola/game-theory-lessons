# Common values and the winner's curse

<p class="lead">In a private-value auction, your value is your own. In a common-value auction everyone wants the same thing, an oil lease, a spectrum license, a company, but nobody knows exactly what it is worth. Each bidder sees a noisy signal, and the mere fact that you won is bad news: it means your signal was probably too high.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
Your signal is unbiased before you bid: on average it equals the true value. But condition on winning. You win when your signal is the highest of everyone's, and the highest of several unbiased signals sits above the truth. So among the times you actually win, your signal systematically overstated the value, and bidding it means overpaying. The cure is to bid as if you already know you have the highest signal, which means shading below it, and shading more the more rivals you face.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The math

<DefinitionAside label="common-value auction and the winner's curse">
All bidders share the same unknown value; each sees a private signal correlated with it. A naive bidder who bids their unbiased signal overpays, because winning is correlated with having the highest, most optimistic signal. Correcting for this, bidders shade below their signal, and shade more as the number of rivals grows.
</DefinitionAside>

Take a common value $V$ with $n$ bidders, each seeing a signal $s_i = V + e_i$ whose noise $e_i$ is symmetric around zero. Your signal is unbiased, so before you bid $\mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i] = s_i$. Now condition on winning. You win when your signal is the highest of the $n$, so your noise is the largest of $n$ draws, which is positive on average. With noise spread evenly over $[-a, a]$, the expected largest of $n$ draws is $a\,\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}$, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[V \mid \text{win}] = s_i - a\,\frac{n-1}{n+1},
$$

below your signal. So bid your signal minus that correction: shade below the signal, and shade more as $n$ grows, since $\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}$ climbs toward $1$.
</Section>

<Section id="reality">
## A note from the real world

<Callout kind="reality-check">
The curse is not just a theorem; people fall for it. In classroom jar-of-coins auctions the winning bid routinely exceeds the true value, and inexperienced bidders in oil-lease and spectrum auctions have famously overpaid. Experienced bidders learn to shade, and to shade harder in a crowded field. The Behavioral unit returns to how bidders learn (or fail to learn) to escape the curse.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Condition on winning">
You see signal $s$, with noise spread $\pm a$ around the true value. Naively bidding $s$ treats your signal as the value. But you win only when $s$ is the highest of the $n$ signals, and the highest signal carries the largest noise, positive on average. The expected largest of $n$ symmetric draws on $[-a, a]$ is $a\,\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}$, so conditional on winning the value is about $s - a\,\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}$.

Put in $a = 10$. Against one rival ($n = 2$) you shade by $10 \cdot \tfrac13 \approx 3.3$ and bid about $s - 3.3$. Against seven rivals ($n = 8$) you shade by $10 \cdot \tfrac79 \approx 7.8$ and bid about $s - 7.8$: the same signal, a lower bid, the larger the field. Bidding your raw signal wins exactly the items you overvalued, which is the curse.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">When bidders share a common value, winning is bad news: your signal was the highest, so it overstated the prize. Shade below your signal, and shade more the more rivals you face.</Callout>
</Section>
