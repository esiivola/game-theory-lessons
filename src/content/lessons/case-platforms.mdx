# Co-opetition and platforms

<p class="lead">Business is not only rivalry. Complementors, firms whose products make yours more valuable, expand the whole pie, and platforms live or die on cross-side network effects. That is why a platform will price one side below cost: cheap access on one side pulls in the other, and the profit follows.</p>

<Section id="why">
## Why did that happen?

<Callout kind="ask">
On a two-sided platform, each side wants to be where the other side is: developers go where the users are, users go where the apps are. So growing one side raises the value of joining the other. A platform exploits this by subsidizing the side that is more price-sensitive or more pivotal, sometimes below cost, to build critical mass, then monetizes the side that will pay to reach it. Pricing each side to cover its own cost, as in a one-sided market, would leave the network stuck below the threshold where it takes off.
</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="math">
## The real strategy

<DefinitionAside label="two-sided markets (Rochet-Tirole)">
A platform serves two groups whose benefits depend on each other's participation (cross-side network effects). The profit-maximizing prices are not cost-based per side; the platform balances the two, often charging one side little or nothing and recovering profit from the other. The classic examples are payment cards (courting cardholders, charging merchants), game consoles (cheap hardware, royalties on games), and app stores (a large cut from developers, free to users).
</DefinitionAside>

Adoption on each side rises with the other side's size, so the two demands reinforce each other. Cutting the price on one side raises its adoption, which raises the value and adoption on the other side, and the platform captures the surplus where it charges. The upshot: the price structure across sides can be highly skewed, and a side may even be paid to join. This is co-opetition in Brandenburger and Nalebuff's sense, where a firm's job is to grow the pie (via complementors and network effects), not only to fight over a fixed one.
</Section>

<Section id="worked">
## Work it out

<WorkedExample title="Subsidize to ignite">
Start with a symmetric price on both sides. Adoption is moderate on each. Now cut the price on side A sharply, even below zero. Side A adoption jumps, and because side B's value rises with side A's size, side B adoption climbs too, without lowering B's price. The platform now earns strong margins on side B while side A, though unprofitable directly, is the magnet that made side B lucrative.

That is why consoles sell hardware near cost and profit on game royalties, and why card networks lavish rewards on shoppers and bill merchants. Treating each side as a standalone business, and insisting it cover its own cost, would miss the cross-side spillover and leave the network below the tipping point where it becomes valuable at all.
</WorkedExample>
</Section>

<Section id="key">
## Key idea

<Callout kind="key-idea">In two-sided markets each side grows with the other, so platforms price one side low, sometimes below cost, to ignite the network and monetize the other. Complementors and network effects expand the pie, which is the essence of co-opetition.</Callout>
</Section>
